Energy: nuclear new-build and the solar boom

Bulgaria is in the middle of two energy build-outs at once: a decade-long nuclear project at Kozloduy and a fast solar and battery expansion. Both create work for foreign suppliers and contractors. They carry very different risks.

October 2026 · This article is in English.

Nuclear: Kozloduy units 7 and 8

Bulgaria plans two Westinghouse AP1000 reactors at Kozloduy, built with Hyundai Engineering & Construction.

Where the project stands

  • An engineering contract was signed in November 2024 between the project company Kozloduy NPP-New Build, Hyundai E&C and Westinghouse, and has since been extended so work is not interrupted (World Nuclear News).
  • An owner's engineer contract, worth what the parties described as hundreds of millions of euros, was signed in December 2025 with Laurentis Energy Partners, its subsidiary Canadian Nuclear Partners and BWXT Canada (World Nuclear News).
  • The main engineering, procurement and construction (EPC) contract had not been signed as of May 2026. Final cost will only be clear once it is. The next steps were European Commission approval, a completed financial model and bank financing documents (Novinite).
  • Target dates differ by source: the energy ministry cited unit 7 in 2035 and unit 8 in 2037 in April 2026; Westinghouse said in May 2026 that it expects the reactors to begin operating in 2037 (World Nuclear News; Novinite).

Local content

The then caretaker energy minister welcomed a Korean commitment to assign 30% of activities to Bulgarian companies and said he wanted the units built at fixed prices, citing past projects where extensions and weak price control led to failure (World Nuclear News). The project reports 36 memoranda of understanding with Bulgarian suppliers (Novinite).

What that means for suppliers

A 30% local share on a project of this scale is a large opportunity for Bulgarian engineering, construction, steel and services firms, and for foreign firms partnering with them. It also concentrates risk: nuclear supply chains demand quality assurance, traceability and security clearances, and payment depends on a contract structure that is not yet final. Bulgaria's previous attempt at new nuclear capacity, at Belene, was cancelled in 2012 after years of spending.

Solar and batteries

Solar

Bulgaria added 1,416 MW of solar in 2025, the third year in a row above 1 GW, bringing cumulative capacity to 5,984 MW. About 90% was utility-scale. Industry forecasts point to more than 2 GW in 2026 (pv magazine).

Batteries

Storage has grown even faster, driven by the RESTORE programme funded from EU recovery money. In April 2025 the energy ministry announced 82 projects totalling 9.7 GWh, backed by €588 million; a second phase added more than 4 GWh. The system operator reported 3,432 MW of batteries installed by May 2026, up from about 200 MWh of storage two years earlier, and Bulgaria now stores a larger share of its electricity production than any other EU member state (IENE, citing Novinite).

The friction

  • Over 1 TWh of solar output, about 3% of consumption, was wasted in 2025 for lack of storage.
  • Midday prices can fall below zero while evening prices reach €250 per MWh.
  • The 864 MW Chaira pumped-storage plant has been offline since April 2022, with full restoration not expected before mid-2027.
  • Interconnections are congested, arbitrage margins are shrinking, and subsidies will expire (IENE).

Where foreign firms fit

  • Equipment and services for solar, batteries and grid upgrades: inverters, transformers, switchgear, operations and maintenance, monitoring.
  • Nuclear supply chain subcontracting, usually through or alongside the main contractors.
  • Industrial power buyers, such as data centres or electro-intensive manufacturing, attracted by midday surplus power and storage.
  • Inspection and maintenance, including thermal drone inspection of solar parks, as fleets age.

Risks to price in

  • Contract and counterparty risk on state-linked projects, where terms can change with governments.
  • Equipment origin. Much solar and battery hardware is Chinese. Some buyers and EU rules increasingly scrutinise this, including forced-labour concerns in solar supply chains and cybersecurity of inverters and battery management systems.
  • Grid and market risk. Revenue models that depend on price spreads or subsidies can change quickly.
  • Permitting and grid connection delays.

What this means for buyers and investors

  • For nuclear work, who is your contractual counterparty, and is your scope tied to an EPC contract that is not yet signed?
  • Can your Bulgarian partner meet nuclear quality-assurance and security requirements?
  • For solar and storage, where were the panels, inverters and batteries made, and who controls their software?
  • Does a project's revenue rely on subsidies or price spreads that may narrow?
  • Who owns the project company, and has ownership changed since permits were issued?
