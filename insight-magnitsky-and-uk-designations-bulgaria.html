US Global Magnitsky and UK anti-corruption designations of Bulgarian persons

Since 2021 the United States and the United Kingdom have sanctioned a number of prominent Bulgarian politicians, officials and businessmen for corruption. These designations matter to buyers well beyond the named individuals, because they extend automatically to companies the individuals own. This note sets out what has been designated and how to screen for it.

October 2026 · This article is in English.

The descriptions below repeat the grounds given by the designating governments. They are allegations by those governments, not court findings, and several of the people named deny wrongdoing.

The designations

June 2021, United States. The US Treasury designated businessman Vassil Bozhkov, former MP and media owner Delyan Peevski, and official Ilko Zhelyazkov under the Global Magnitsky programme, together with 64 companies: 58 linked to Bozhkov and 6 linked to Peevski. The State Department separately designated Peevski, Zhelyazkov and three former officials, Alexander Manolev, Petar Haralampiev and Krasimir Tomov, with some family members, under a visa-ban authority (Sofia Globe).

February 2023, United States. The Treasury designated five more people under the order that implements the Global Magnitsky Act (US Treasury):

  • Rumen Ovcharov, former energy minister;
  • Aleksandar Nikolov and Ivan Genov, both former chief executives of the Kozloduy nuclear power plant, in connection with alleged kickbacks on energy contracts;
  • Nikolay Malinov, former MP and leader of the Russophiles National Movement;
  • Vladislav Goranov, former finance minister.

Five companies were designated alongside them: four linked to Malinov, including two Russophile political organisations, and one linked to Goranov.

February 2023, United Kingdom. On the same day, the UK designated Bozhkov, Peevski and Zhelyazkov under its Global Anti-Corruption sanctions regime (RFE/RL).

Designations can change. Check the current OFAC and OFSI lists, not this note, before acting.

Why this reaches further than the names

  • Ownership rules. Under US sanctions, any entity owned 50% or more, directly or indirectly, by one or more designated persons is blocked even if it is not named (Sofia Globe). The UK applies a similar test based on ownership or control.
  • Control without ownership. Companies run through relatives, nominees or long-standing associates may not meet the 50% test but still create reputational and legal risk, especially under UK "control" rules.
  • Indirect exposure. A supplier that is not itself blocked may sell through, buy from, bank with or lease from a designated person's company.
  • No EU equivalent. The EU has not designated these individuals, so EU banks and Bulgarian companies are not legally barred from dealing with them. US and UK companies, and anyone transacting in US dollars or through UK firms, are.

That last point creates an awkward split: a Bulgarian supplier can lawfully do business with a company that its UK or US customer is forbidden to touch.

How to screen

  1. Screen the supplier, its owners and its managers against OFAC's list and the UK sanctions list, using a service that applies ownership rules, not just name matches.
  2. Map ownership to individuals using the Commercial Register, including companies where the same managers appear.
  3. Check counterparties that matter: the supplier's landlord, main bank, largest customers, logistics providers and any agent.
  4. Search Bulgarian-language media, where links between companies and designated individuals are often reported long before they appear in databases.
  5. Record the result and the date, and repeat it. Ownership changes, and companies can be restructured to fall below thresholds.

What this means for buyers

  • Have you screened your supplier's owners, managers and key counterparties against both US and UK lists?
  • Does your screening apply the 50% ownership and UK control tests, or only exact name matches?
  • Is your supplier lawfully dealing with a designated person's company under Bulgarian law, in a way your own law forbids you to touch?
  • If a match appears, do you know who in your organisation decides, and whether you must report it?
