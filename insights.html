Outlook

Our annual view of Bulgaria as a supplier base: country and counterparty risk, a sector scorecard, corridors, key dates and scenarios.

Outlook

Bulgarian Supplier Outlook 2026

Bulgaria enters 2027 as a lower-risk country but not yet a lower-risk supplier base. The euro, Schengen and a probable exit from the FATF grey list have removed macro and banking frictions. Sanctions enforcement, ownership transparency and governance of state-linked companies are still catching up, so counterparty checks matter as much as they did in 2025.

Guides

How to check a Bulgarian company in 30 minutes

Bulgaria's company register is one of the most open in Europe: the record, the board and the filed accounts are free to read without an account. The obstacles are language, a few blind spots, and knowing which signals matter.

→Guides

Who really owns a Bulgarian supplier?

Knowing who ultimately owns and controls a supplier is the core of sanctions and diversion checks. Since a 2022 EU court ruling, beneficial-ownership registers across Europe have become harder to read, and Bulgaria is changing its rules too. Here is what a foreign buyer can still see, and how to fill the gaps.

→Guides

What a supplier verification report should contain

A supplier verification report is only useful if a compliance officer can file it, an auditor can test it and a decision-maker can act on it. Most fail one of those tests. This note sets out the structure we use and the questions a buyer should ask of any report, ours included.

→Guides

Public procurement in Bulgaria for foreign bidders

EU, defence and energy money is flowing into Bulgarian public contracts, and foreign companies are entitled to bid on equal terms. In practice, competition is thin in many tenders, and the procedural details trip up newcomers. This note covers how the system works, where it goes wrong, and how to read a tender before spending money on a bid.

→Guides

Contract enforcement in Bulgaria: courts, arbitration and realistic timelines

A contract with a Bulgarian supplier or customer is only as good as your ability to enforce it. Bulgarian courts work, but slowly, and collecting on a judgment can take longer than winning it. Foreign companies should decide how disputes will be resolved before they sign.

Risk briefs

Where sanctions, ownership, governance and illicit-trade risks sit, and how they reach a buyer's supply chain.

Risk briefs

Red flags for re-export through the Balkans and the Black Sea

Bulgaria sits on the EU's land border with Turkey and on the Black Sea, which makes it a working route to the Caucasus and Central Asia. Since 2022 the same routes have been used to move controlled goods to Russia. Buyers and sellers who know the warning signs can price that risk instead of discovering it.

→Risk briefs

State-owned companies in defence and energy: how to read governance risk

Many of Bulgaria's largest counterparties in energy, transport and defence are owned by the state. They are often solid businesses with real assets. Their management, pricing and commitments can also change when the government does. Buyers and investors should price that.

→Risk briefs

Russia exposure map of the Bulgarian economy

Bulgaria has cut much of its dependence on Russia since 2022, but the remaining links sit in places that matter to buyers: fuel, gas transit, and a long tail of ownership and trade relationships. This note maps where Russian exposure still sits and how it can reach a Western supply chain.

→Risk briefs

Bulgaria and the FATF grey list: what it means for counterparties

Since October 2023 Bulgaria has been on the Financial Action Task Force's list of jurisdictions under increased monitoring, usually called the grey list. A decision on removing it is expected at the FATF plenary ending on 30 October 2026. Whichever way that goes, the listing has shaped how banks and compliance teams treat Bulgarian counterparties, and the reasons for it still matter.

→Risk briefs

US Global Magnitsky and UK anti-corruption designations of Bulgarian persons

Since 2021 the United States and the United Kingdom have sanctioned a number of prominent Bulgarian politicians, officials and businessmen for corruption. These designations matter to buyers well beyond the named individuals, because they extend automatically to companies the individuals own. This note sets out what has been designated and how to screen for it.

→Risk briefs

Excise and illicit trade: tobacco, fuel and brand protection

Excise goods (tobacco, fuel and alcohol) carry high taxes, which makes them a constant target for smuggling and fraud. Bulgaria's own market is relatively clean on cigarettes, but it is a notable source of cheap cigarettes that turn up illegally elsewhere in Europe, and fuel fraud has a long history. For brand owners, distributors and logistics firms, the risk is being used as a link in someone else's scheme.

Regulatory

EU and Bulgarian rules that change what buyers and exporters must do: export controls, carbon, deforestation, investment screening, sanctions enforcement.

Regulatory

Dual-use exports from Bulgaria: who licenses, how long it takes, and common mistakes

Many Bulgarian manufacturers make goods that have both civilian and military uses without thinking of themselves as exporters of controlled items. Sensors, optics, machine tools, electronics and certain chemicals can all need a licence to leave the EU. This note sets out how the Bulgarian system works and where companies usually go wrong.

→Regulatory

CBAM for south-east European supply chains

Since 1 January 2026, importers bringing steel, aluminium, cement, fertilisers, electricity or hydrogen into the EU face a carbon charge at the border. For buyers sourcing these goods from Turkey, Serbia, North Macedonia or further east through Bulgaria, the Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanism is now a cost line, not a reporting exercise.

→Regulatory

The EU deforestation rules and Balkan timber and furniture

The EU Deforestation Regulation will require companies selling wood products in the EU to prove where the wood came from and that it was harvested legally. Bulgaria is classed as low risk for deforestation, but its record on illegal logging means buyers should not treat "low risk" as "no checks".

→Regulatory

One year of the euro: what changed for Bulgarian suppliers

Bulgaria adopted the euro on 1 January 2026. For foreign buyers, most of the effect is good news: no exchange-rate risk, simpler pricing and payments. A few transition rules still matter, especially in contracts and company documents.

→Regulatory

Chinese investment in Bulgaria and the Western Balkans, and how screening works

Chinese capital in south-east Europe is concentrated in Serbia, while Bulgaria is seeing a new, smaller wave tied to cars and automotive parts. Since July 2025 Bulgaria has screened foreign investment from outside the EU. For buyers and investors, the questions are who controls the supplier, what it depends on, and whether a deal needs approval.

→Regulatory

Who enforces sanctions in Bulgaria

EU sanctions apply directly in every member state, but enforcement is national. In Bulgaria the system is spread across several agencies, the criminal law has lagged behind EU requirements, and the rules for companies changed in 2025. This note sets out who does what and what is changing.

Sector deep dives

Bulgarian industries one at a time: what is made, who makes it, and what to check before buying.

Sector deep dives

Bulgaria's automotive and electronics suppliers: inside most European cars, rarely on the label

Bulgaria makes no cars of its own but supplies sensors, wiring and electronics to most of Europe's carmakers. The sector is the biggest pull for foreign manufacturing investment, but it is tied to a European car industry in decline and is increasingly drawing Chinese suppliers.

→Sector deep dives

Bulgaria's defence industry: history, today, and outlook

Bulgaria has a long-established ammunition and small-arms industry that is now attracting Western investment and EU money, but buyers need to price a history of re-export, state-owned governance and patchy ownership data.

→Sector deep dives

Software and IT in Bulgaria: what the evidence shows, and what buyers should ask

Software and IT services is Bulgaria's best-documented growth sector, but its strengths and its risks both sit in a small number of foreign-owned employers and a tight talent pool.

→Sector deep dives

Rose oil, lavender and honey: authenticity risk in Bulgarian agri-food

Bulgaria's best-known natural products, rose oil from the Kazanlak valley, lavender oil and honey, sell on origin and purity. That is exactly what makes them targets for adulteration and mislabelling. For buyers, verifying the supplier and verifying the product are two separate checks.

→Sector deep dives

Drones and dual-use electronics on the Eastern Flank

Europe wants drones and counter-drone systems quickly, and much of the hardware inside them still comes from China. Bulgaria has small but real drone makers and a long record in military electronics. For buyers, the questions are where the components come from, where the products go, and who controls the company.

→Sector deep dives

Energy: nuclear new-build and the solar boom

Bulgaria is in the middle of two energy build-outs at once: a decade-long nuclear project at Kozloduy and a fast solar and battery expansion. Both create work for foreign suppliers and contractors. They carry very different risks.

→Sector deep dives

Pharmaceuticals and generics in Bulgaria: exports, Russia and supply-chain integrity

Bulgaria has a long-established generics industry, a large wholesale trade and a fast-growing clinical-trials business. Its export profile still carries a heavy Russian weighting, and its wholesale sector re-exports medicines across the EU. For buyers and partners, the questions are product integrity, distribution routes and customers.

→Sector deep dives

Metals and mining: Bulgaria's copper economy and the EU raw-materials push

Copper is Bulgaria's largest export, ahead of arms and grain. One smelter at Pirdop dominates the trade, mines supply part of the ore, and the rest is imported. With the EU treating copper as a strategic raw material, the sector is attracting public money and closer scrutiny of where ore comes from.

→Sector deep dives

Chemicals and fertilisers: gas, carbon costs and Russian competition

Bulgaria's chemical industry is built on cheap gas, salt and Black Sea access: nitrogen fertilisers at Devnya and Dimitrovgrad, and soda ash at Devnya. Since 2022 it has faced three shocks at once: expensive gas, rising carbon costs, and EU tariffs that reshape competition with Russian fertiliser. Buyers need to understand all three.

→Sector deep dives

Space and satellites: Bulgaria's fastest-growing dual-use industry

Bulgaria now hosts one of Europe's fastest-growing satellite manufacturers. Satellites are inherently dual-use: the same platform can carry an imaging payload for agriculture or for military surveillance. That makes the sector a strong example of high-value Bulgarian manufacturing, and of why export controls and customer checks matter.

→Sector deep dives

Grain and agri-exports through Varna and Burgas

Bulgaria is a significant exporter of wheat, sunflower and other crops, and its Black Sea ports also handle neighbours' grain. Since 2022 the trade has been shaped by Ukrainian exports, farmer protests and the risk of Russian-origin or stolen Ukrainian grain entering legitimate supply chains. Buyers need to know where each cargo really came from.

→Sector deep dives

Data centres and AI compute in Bulgaria: power, chips and security

Bulgaria is trying to move from software outsourcing into hosting and running compute itself. A European AI factory is planned in Sofia, a global data-centre operator has entered the market, and cheap midday solar power is in surplus. The constraints are chip-export rules, power and grid limits, and a physical-security environment that has become less forgiving.

Regional briefs

Bulgaria's neighbours and corridors, from Romania and Greece to Turkey, the Caucasus, Central Asia and Ukraine.

Regional briefs

Bulgaria on the Silk Road, then and now

Bulgaria was a corridor on routes that branched from the Silk Road, was largely bypassed by China's Belt and Road, and now sits at the EU end of the Middle Corridor. Each time, location brought traffic, but the rules of trade decided who profited.

→Regional briefs

Is Bulgaria a Balkan Tiger? What it takes, who did it, and what is missing

Bulgaria now has the anchors past tigers had (EU market, euro, low taxes), but not the trust that turned those anchors into a decade of fast growth; geography, from the Silk Road to the Belt and Road, has never closed that gap by itself.

→Regional briefs

Kapitan Andreevo and the EU–Turkey land border

Kapitan Andreevo, on the Bulgarian side, and Kapıkule, on the Turkish side, form one of the busiest land freight crossings in Europe. For buyers moving goods between Turkey and the EU by road, delay here is a cost to plan for, not an exception.

→Regional briefs

The Middle Corridor, a reality check

The Middle Corridor, the route from China and Central Asia across the Caspian Sea, the Caucasus and the Black Sea to Europe, is often presented as the new Silk Road. Volumes are growing fast. They are also still small, and Bulgaria is one of several Black Sea landing points competing for them.

→Regional briefs

Beyond the EU border: sourcing from Serbia and North Macedonia

Serbia and North Macedonia sit next to Bulgaria, share much of its industrial history and often quote lower prices. For a Western buyer, the difference is not geography but rules: a supplier one border west of Sofia operates outside the EU's legal framework, and in Serbia's case outside its sanctions regime.

→Regional briefs

Bulgaria vs Romania as a production base

Bulgaria and Romania joined the EU together in 2007, share the Danube border and compete for the same nearshoring investment. Romania offers scale; Bulgaria offers lower taxes, the euro and a tidier public balance sheet. The right choice depends on what you need most.

→Regional briefs

The Greece–Bulgaria corridor: Alexandroupolis, gas and the rail link to the Black Sea

Greece and Bulgaria are building a north–south corridor that connects Aegean ports with the Black Sea and the Danube without passing through the Bosporus. It already carries gas and military logistics; rail freight is slower to arrive. For buyers, it offers an alternative route into and out of Bulgaria, and a way to diversify away from the Turkish land border.

→Regional briefs

Turkey as partner and competitor

Turkey is Bulgaria's largest neighbour, a top trading partner, a growing investor and, increasingly, a competitor for European manufacturing and defence work. It is also the main land route between the EU and the Middle East, the Caucasus and Central Asia. Buyers who source from or through Bulgaria need a view on Turkey.

→Regional briefs

Georgia, Armenia and Azerbaijan: opportunity and re-export risk in the South Caucasus

The South Caucasus is where Bulgaria's Black Sea links lead east: ferries to Georgia, gas from Azerbaijan, and the Middle Corridor towards Central Asia. These are growing markets in their own right. Since 2022 they have also become the region where EU goods most visibly reappear in Russia. Both facts are true at once, and buyers need to handle both.

→Regional briefs

Central Asia as markets, not just transit

Kazakhstan, Uzbekistan and their neighbours are often discussed only as stops on the Middle Corridor. They are also fast-moving markets and suppliers of raw materials the EU wants. Since April 2026 one of them, Kyrgyzstan, has become the first country the EU has targeted with trade restrictions for helping Russia evade sanctions. Opportunity and risk sit side by side.

→Regional briefs

Ukraine reconstruction from Bulgaria

Rebuilding Ukraine will be one of the largest construction and engineering programmes in Europe for a decade. Bulgaria sits close by, across the Black Sea and the Danube, with energy, transport and construction firms keen to take part. Politics, security and the integrity of procurement will decide who benefits.
