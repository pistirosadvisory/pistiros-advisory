Russia exposure map of the Bulgarian economy

Bulgaria has cut much of its dependence on Russia since 2022, but the remaining links sit in places that matter to buyers: fuel, gas transit, and a long tail of ownership and trade relationships. This note maps where Russian exposure still sits and how it can reach a Western supply chain.

October 2026 · This article is in English.

Fuel: the Burgas refinery

The largest single exposure is Lukoil Neftohim Burgas, the country's only crude oil refinery, with capacity reported at about 7–8 million tonnes of crude a year (Novinite).

In late 2025 the United States sanctioned Lukoil, and the sanctions extended to its Bulgarian operations. Bulgaria responded by creating a "special commercial manager" mechanism for sanctioned strategic assets and appointing a manager to run Lukoil's Bulgarian companies. Reports have named different people in that role, so check who holds it today (Novinite).

The refinery operates under licences that keep being renewed for short periods:

  • United States. On 15 October 2026 OFAC issued General License 130B, covering certain transactions with Lukoil's Bulgarian subsidiaries, including the refinery, fuel stations and aviation and marine fuel suppliers, until 24 April 2027. The licence does not lift sanctions on Lukoil itself (BTA).
  • United Kingdom. In August 2026 the UK extended its waiver for the Bulgarian subsidiaries (Lukoil Bulgaria, Lukoil Neftohim Burgas, Lukoil Aviation Bulgaria and Lukoil Bunker Bulgaria) to 29 October 2026, to line it up with the US licence (S&P Global). As of 25 October 2026 we had not seen a further UK extension; check whether it has been renewed.

The government has discussed buying the refinery but, as of May 2026, the finance minister said it was not on the formal agenda (Novinite).

Why it matters to buyers: any contract that touches Burgas refinery products, fuel cards at Lukoil stations, or aviation and bunker fuel in Bulgaria depends on licences that expire every few months. A UK or US company should confirm the current licence covers its activity before each renewal date, not once.

Gas

Russia's Gazprom stopped supplying Bulgaria's state gas company in April 2022, after Bulgaria refused to pay in roubles. Bulgaria now imports through the Greece–Bulgaria interconnector, LNG via Greece and Turkey, and Azerbaijani gas.

Russian gas still crosses Bulgaria in transit. Gazprom Export uses almost all of TurkStream's capacity on the Bulgarian section to supply Serbia and Hungary (Capital). In September 2025 Bulgaria said it would end Russian gas transit in 2026 (Ukrainska Pravda), EU sanctions require long-term Russian LNG contracts to end by 1 January 2027 (Capital), and separate EU rules are phasing out Russian pipeline gas. The transit route links Bulgaria's gas operator to Gazprom until transit stops.

Nuclear

Bulgaria's two operating reactors at Kozloduy are Soviet-designed. Bulgaria began moving away from Russian fuel in 2024, with US-supplied fuel loaded at one unit, and the new units 7 and 8 will use US technology. Remaining Russian links are mainly in spare parts and service know-how for the existing units.

Ownership and trade

Beyond energy, Russian links show up as:

  • Company ownership by Russian or Russian-linked individuals, sometimes through Cyprus or other holding jurisdictions.
  • Property on the Black Sea coast, where Russian citizens were large buyers before 2022.
  • Former trading relationships, including legacy defence sales, pharmaceutical exports and machinery.
  • Transit, where goods have been routed through Bulgaria towards Russia (see our red-flags note).

These are not reasons to avoid Bulgarian suppliers. They are reasons to check ownership, customers and counterparties rather than assume an EU address settles the question.

What to check

ExposureQuestion for your supplier or counterparty
FuelDo you buy fuel from Lukoil entities, and under which licence?
Gas and energyIs your energy supply contracted through a party linked to Russian suppliers?
OwnershipDoes any owner, director or parent company have Russian or Belarusian ties?
CustomersWhat share of sales went to Russia, Belarus or transit hubs before and after 2022?
BankingDo you hold accounts or receive payments through banks with Russian exposure?
LogisticsDo your shipments touch Russian-owned forwarders, vessels or warehouses?

What this means for buyers

  • Does your activity in Bulgaria depend on a sanctions licence, and do you track its expiry?
  • Have you screened owners and directors, not just the company, for Russian links?
  • Did your supplier's sales to Russia stop in 2022, or shift to intermediaries?
  • Would a lapse in the Lukoil licences disrupt your logistics or fuel supply?
