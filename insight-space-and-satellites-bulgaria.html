Space and satellites: Bulgaria's fastest-growing dual-use industry

Bulgaria now hosts one of Europe's fastest-growing satellite manufacturers. Satellites are inherently dual-use: the same platform can carry an imaging payload for agriculture or for military surveillance. That makes the sector a strong example of high-value Bulgarian manufacturing, and of why export controls and customer checks matter.

October 2026 · This article is in English.

EnduroSat

Sofia-based EnduroSat builds satellite platforms, the "bus" that carries a customer's payload. Its growth has been rapid:

  • May 2025: a €43 million round led by Founders Fund (European Spaceflight).
  • October 2025: a further $104 million, alongside the opening of a 17,500 m² facility in Sofia, up from 3,200 m². At full capacity the plant is designed to produce two 200–500 kg satellites a day (European Spaceflight).
  • September 2026: $205 million led by Riot Ventures and Atreides Management, with the European Innovation Council, Google Ventures, Founders Fund and others, reported as the largest funding round for a Bulgarian company. The money will expand production, open a US manufacturing facility and build larger spacecraft. Earth-observation operator Vantor selected EnduroSat to supply buses for 24 satellites in its imaging constellation, with CACI building the imaging payloads (Payload).

The company's own materials describe the planned Bulgarian site as an EU defence hub. That positions Bulgaria inside European and allied space-security supply chains, not just commercial ones.

Why this matters beyond one company

  • High-value manufacturing. Satellite production is the opposite of the low-cost assembly model that has defined much of Bulgarian industry. It needs engineers, clean rooms and supply chains in electronics, optics and precision machining.
  • A supplier ecosystem. A plant designed for up to two satellites a day needs local suppliers of machined parts, harnesses, test services and logistics. Some will be small Bulgarian firms new to space-grade quality and export rules.
  • Dual-use by default. Many satellite components, ground-station equipment and some software fall under EU dual-use controls. Earth-observation imagery and services can be restricted for certain destinations and users.

Risks to manage

  • Export controls. Satellites and their components often need export licences outside the EU. Transfers of controlled technology, including design files sent abroad, count as exports.
  • US content. Products that include US-origin parts or technology can fall under US export rules, which reach re-exports from Bulgaria. A new US facility makes this relationship closer.
  • End users. Imaging constellations serve governments, defence agencies and commercial buyers. Know who ultimately uses the data and the hardware.
  • Component sourcing. As with drones, some electronic components come from China, which may be unacceptable to defence customers.
  • Growth risk. Very fast scale-up strains quality systems, hiring and supplier vetting.

Who the buyers are

  • Constellation operators buying platforms.
  • Defence and government agencies buying capacity or data.
  • Primes integrating Bulgarian-built buses into larger systems.
  • Investors and suppliers joining the ecosystem.

What this means for buyers and suppliers

  • If you buy from a Bulgarian space supplier, which items are controlled, and who holds the licences?
  • Does the product include US-origin content, and has its US export status been classified?
  • If you supply a Bulgarian space company, do your own parts or data fall under export controls?
  • Who are the end users of the satellites and their data?
  • Can the supplier's quality and compliance systems keep up with its growth?
