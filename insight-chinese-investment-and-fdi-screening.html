Chinese investment in Bulgaria and the Western Balkans, and how screening works

Chinese capital in south-east Europe is concentrated in Serbia, while Bulgaria is seeing a new, smaller wave tied to cars and automotive parts. Since July 2025 Bulgaria has screened foreign investment from outside the EU. For buyers and investors, the questions are who controls the supplier, what it depends on, and whether a deal needs approval.

October 2026 · This article is in English.

The regional picture: Serbia dominates

The Vienna Institute for International Economic Studies estimates that Chinese direct investment stock in the Western Balkans rose from 0.2% of regional GDP in 2015 to 4.2% in 2024. Almost all of it is in Serbia, which held about €5.4 billion of the region's €5.6 billion Chinese stock in 2023, close to 9% of its GDP. Elsewhere it is below 1% of GDP (wiiw).

Older Serbian investments sit in mining and heavy industry: the Smederevo steel plant, the Bor copper mine and a tyre plant. Newer ones are moving into car parts, appliances and renewable energy. The same study notes links to pollution and labour-rights problems at several projects, and that EU due-diligence and state-aid rules cannot be enforced outside the EU (wiiw).

For a buyer, that means a Serbian supplier is more likely than a Bulgarian one to sit in a Chinese-owned supply chain or depend on a Chinese-owned input.

Bulgaria: cars, parts and a reopened plant

Chinese activity in Bulgaria is smaller and more recent. A November 2025 analysis by the Central European Institute of Asian Studies found (CEIAS):

  • Chinese car brands held 3.2% of the Bulgarian car market in September 2025, double a year earlier, with Dongfeng, Geely and others expanding and more brands expected in 2026.
  • Great Wall Motor was finalising talks to restart vehicle assembly at the former plant near Lovech, mostly from imported parts.
  • A Chinese aluminium auto-parts maker, ZS Europe, is opening two plants near Plovdiv worth about €16 million, supplying European carmakers.
  • An unnamed Chinese company is building a plant in Sevlievo to make electrical transformers for the auto-parts industry.

These are modest sums compared with Western investment in the Plovdiv cluster, but they add Chinese owners to supply chains that European carmakers and defence-adjacent electronics buyers use.

How Bulgaria screens foreign investment

Bulgaria was one of the last EU states without investment screening. Amendments to the Investment Promotion Act adopted in March 2024 created a regime that became applicable on 22 July 2025, when its implementing rules were published (Wolf Theiss). Key features:

  • Who is caught. Investors from outside the EU, including companies in which a non-EU state holds a stake or has provided significant financing.
  • Thresholds. Acquiring at least 10% of a company, or investments above €2 million. There is no minimum for oil and petroleum activities, investors from Russia or Belarus, deals that may affect national security, or investors with non-EU state links.
  • Sectors. Critical infrastructure (energy, transport, water, health, communications, media, finance), critical and dual-use technologies (AI, semiconductors, robotics, aerospace, defence, energy storage, nuclear), critical inputs including raw materials and food, sensitive data, and media.
  • Process. Mandatory approval before closing. Notifications go to the InvestBulgaria Agency, and a screening council decides within 45 days, which can be extended while the European Commission comments. Silence is treated as approval.

The regime is new and there is little published practice. Penalties for failing to notify, and how strictly "state links" is read, should be checked with counsel.

What this means in practice

  • For investors buying into a Bulgarian company with non-EU money, including through funds with Chinese, Gulf or Turkish state-linked investors, screening may apply even to minority stakes.
  • For buyers of Bulgarian components, a recent change of ownership to a non-EU investor may have gone through screening, or should have. Ask.
  • For defence and critical-infrastructure supply chains, Chinese ownership or control of a supplier may be unacceptable under your own customer's rules, regardless of Bulgarian law.

What this means for buyers

  • Does your supplier, or its parent, have Chinese or other non-EU state-linked owners or lenders?
  • Has its ownership changed since July 2025, and was the deal screened?
  • Do any of its critical inputs come from Chinese-owned plants in Serbia or elsewhere?
  • Would your end customer, especially in defence or energy, accept that ownership?
