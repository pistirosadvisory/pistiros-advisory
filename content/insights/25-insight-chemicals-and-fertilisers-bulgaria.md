# Chemicals and fertilisers: gas, carbon costs and Russian competition

Summary: Bulgaria's chemical industry is built on cheap gas, salt and Black Sea access: nitrogen fertilisers at Devnya and Dimitrovgrad, and soda ash at Devnya. Since 2022 it has faced three shocks at once: expensive gas, rising carbon costs, and EU tariffs that reshape competition with Russian fertiliser. Buyers need to understand all three.
Published: October 2026
Date: 2026-10-25
Category: sectors

## The main producers

- **Agropolychim, Devnya.** Nitrogen and phosphate fertilisers, with its own ammonia plant ([GEM](https://www.gem.wiki/Devnya_Ammonia_Plant)), near Varna port.
- **Neochim, Dimitrovgrad.** Ammonia and nitrogen fertilisers. Described in 2022 as the country's only fertiliser plant producing ammonia at the time ([BNT](https://bntnews.bg/news/natural-gas-crisis-industries-shut-down-for-lack-of-fuel-1306601news.html)).
- **Solvay Sodi, Devnya.** Soda ash (sodium carbonate) for glass, detergents and chemicals, described by Solvay as its largest European soda-ash plant. Bulgaria is the EU's second-largest exporter of carbonate compounds after Germany, worth about €325 million in 2024, mainly to Turkey, Egypt and Saudi Arabia ([Capital](https://www.capital.bg/en/economy/2025/05/29/4788001_metals_munitions_and_rice_a_look_inside_bulgarias/)).

## Shock one: gas

Gas is both fuel and raw material for ammonia. In August 2022 Neochim stopped production because of gas prices. Its chief executive said the plant needed about 12,100 MWh of gas a day and could not run on 15% less; 61 workers were laid off ([BNT](https://bntnews.bg/news/natural-gas-crisis-industries-shut-down-for-lack-of-fuel-1306601news.html)). Agropolychim returned to full capacity after overhauling facilities the same month ([SeeNews](https://seenews.com/news/bulgarias-agropolychim-cuts-reliance-on-natgas-for-production-794835)).

Bulgaria no longer buys Russian gas directly and relies on Azerbaijani gas, LNG and the interconnector with Greece. The 2026 Gulf energy shock has again pushed up gas costs. For buyers, the lesson is supply continuity: nitrogen producers can stop with little notice when gas spikes.

## Shock two: carbon

Fertiliser producers in the EU pay for emissions under the Emissions Trading System. Since January 2026 imported fertilisers carry a matching charge under the Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanism, which narrows the gap with producers outside the EU that pay no carbon price. Soda ash is not covered by CBAM, but its producer still pays ETS costs at home.

## Shock three: Russian competition

From July 2025 the EU began phasing in duties on nitrogen fertilisers from Russia and Belarus, on top of the existing 6.5% import duty. For nitrogen fertilisers the extra charge is €40 a tonne from July 2025, €60 from July 2026 and €80 from July 2027, jumping to €315 from July 2028; some compound fertilisers reach €430 a tonne ([Kpler](https://kpler.com/blog/eus-staged-fertiliser-tariffs-to-increase-costs-for-farmers-through-2028)). Check the regulation for the exact product list.

For Bulgarian producers that is a protective shift. For farmers and traders, it raises prices and pushes supply toward other sources such as Egypt and Algeria.

## Where the risks sit

- **Origin fraud.** As duties on Russian fertiliser rise, the incentive to relabel it grows. Russian or Belarusian product routed through Turkey, the Caucasus or Central Asia and declared as another origin is a known risk for traders.
- **Inputs.** Phosphate rock, potash and ammonia are traded globally. Check where a producer's inputs come from.
- **Ownership.** Chemical plants in the region have changed hands repeatedly since privatisation. Confirm who controls a supplier today.
- **Environmental liabilities.** Old sites carry legacy contamination, emissions permits and community concerns.
- **Dual-use chemicals.** Some industrial chemicals and precursors are export-controlled. A chemicals trader with unusual customers deserves questions.

## What this means for buyers

- If you buy Bulgarian fertiliser or chemicals, how exposed is supply to a gas price spike, and what contingency does the producer have?
- If you trade fertiliser, can you prove the origin of every cargo, especially product arriving through Turkey or the Black Sea?
- Does your supplier pass through its carbon costs, and how will CBAM change competing import prices?
- Have you checked current ownership and environmental permits?
