# What a supplier verification report should contain

Summary: A supplier verification report is only useful if a compliance officer can file it, an auditor can test it and a decision-maker can act on it. Most fail one of those tests. This note sets out the structure we use and the questions a buyer should ask of any report, ours included.
Published: October 2026
Date: 2026-10-25
Category: guides

## What a report is for

A buyer commissions a verification report to answer one question: can we trade with this company, and on what conditions? The report has to support that decision now and defend it later, if a regulator, auditor or court asks why the buyer went ahead.

That second use shapes everything. A report that states conclusions without showing evidence, or mixes what was proven with what was guessed, may read well but will not survive scrutiny.

## The structure

**1. Scope and limitations.** Who the subject is, who commissioned the report, the date, which sources were checked and which were not. If the report did not include a site visit, interviews or paid databases, it should say so on the first page.

**2. Verdict on one page.** A clear rating (for example red, amber or green), the three findings that drive it, and the three actions that would change it. A busy reader should be able to stop here.

**3. Entity profile.** Registration number, legal form, history, address checks, activity, size from filed accounts, and whether the company appears to produce what it sells or to trade it.

**4. Ownership and control.** A diagram from registered shareholders to the individuals who ultimately own or control the company, with each link sourced. Director history, recent changes, and other companies the same people control. Where ownership could not be confirmed, the gap is a finding, not a footnote.

**5. Sanctions and adverse media.** The company, its owners, its managers and its main counterparties screened against EU, UK and US lists, with the date and list version. Adverse media in the local language as well as English, each item dated and sourced.

**6. Export-control posture.** Whether the supplier's products are likely to be controlled (stated as an indication, not a legal classification), which licences it holds, and whether it has a written compliance programme. The European Commission's 2019 recommendation on internal compliance programmes sets out seven core elements: management commitment, organisation and resources, training, transaction screening, audits and corrective action, record-keeping, and physical and information security ([EUR-Lex](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reco/2019/1318/oj/eng)). A supplier with none of these in writing is a supplier that relies on luck.

**7. End use and diversion risk.** Where the supplier's customers are, whether it sells through intermediaries, how it checks end users, and whether its non-EU contracts carry the clauses EU sanctions law requires.

**8. Certifications.** Each certificate the supplier claims, checked against the issuing body's own records. A certificate on a website is a claim; a confirmation from the registrar is evidence.

**9. Payment and counterparty risk.** Banking relationships, use of third-party payers, and any unusual payment routes, where these are in scope.

**10. Findings register.** One row per finding: what was found, the evidence, how strong the evidence is, the risk it creates, and the recommended action.

**11. Remediation plan.** What the supplier or buyer should do in 30, 90 and 180 days, and who owns each step.

**12. Appendices.** Method, a source list with access dates, and an evidence log recording when each source was captured.

## Grading the evidence

The single most useful discipline in a report is separating what is known from what is inferred. We grade each finding:

- **Confirmed:** shown directly by an official record or primary document.
- **Probable:** supported by several independent sources but not confirmed by an official record.
- **Possible:** a single source, an indirect indicator, or a pattern that needs explanation.

A report that says a director "is linked to" a sanctioned company without saying how, or on what evidence, has not done its job. Neither has one that buries a confirmed problem in the same language as a speculative one.

## What a report cannot do

- It cannot give legal advice or a legal classification of goods. Those belong with counsel.
- It cannot prove a negative. "No adverse findings" means none were found in the sources checked, nothing more.
- It goes stale. Ownership, management and sanctions lists change; a report is a snapshot as of its date.

## Questions to ask of any report

- Does page one state what was not checked?
- Can you trace every finding to a dated source?
- Are confirmed facts clearly separated from inference?
- Does the ownership section reach real people, or stop at another company?
- Were sources checked in the local language?
- Were certifications confirmed with the issuer?
- Is there a clear recommendation, and what would change it?
- Who prepared it, and do they have any interest in the deal going ahead?

The last question matters most. A report paid for by the party that benefits from a clean result deserves a second look. We take fixed fees only, broker no access, and do not act for both sides of a transaction.

We do not give legal advice. Licensing, classification and sanctions questions belong with independent counsel.
