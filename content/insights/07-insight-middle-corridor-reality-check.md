# The Middle Corridor, a reality check

Summary: The Middle Corridor, the route from China and Central Asia across the Caspian Sea, the Caucasus and the Black Sea to Europe, is often presented as the new Silk Road. Volumes are growing fast. They are also still small, and Bulgaria is one of several Black Sea landing points competing for them.
Published: October 2026
Date: 2026-10-25
Category: regional

## What the corridor is

The Trans-Caspian International Transport Route runs by rail from China through Kazakhstan, by ship across the Caspian to Azerbaijan, by rail through Georgia, and then either by land through Turkey or by sea across the Black Sea to Bulgaria, Romania and beyond. It avoids Russia, which is why interest rose sharply after 2022.

## How big it really is

- **2024:** about 4.5 million tonnes, up 62% on the previous year ([Railmarket](https://railmarket.com/news/freight-rail/42407-transit-volumes-on-the-middle-corridor-forecast-to-quadruple-by-end-of-decade)).
- **2025:** growth slowed. January to September volumes were about 2.65 million tonnes, up 6%; the full-year expectation was about 5.2 million tonnes, of which about 2.5 million tonnes was dry cargo (roughly 96,000 containers measured in TEU) and 1.7 million tonnes oil ([Railmarket](https://railmarket.com/news/freight-rail/42407-transit-volumes-on-the-middle-corridor-forecast-to-quadruple-by-end-of-decade)).
- **Forecasts:** a report attributed to Boston Consulting Group projects volumes three to four times higher by the end of the decade, with capacity of up to 10 million tonnes a year by 2027 ([Railmarket](https://railmarket.com/news/freight-rail/42407-transit-volumes-on-the-middle-corridor-forecast-to-quadruple-by-end-of-decade)).

For scale, a 2025 study for the Netherlands Enterprise Agency puts Middle Corridor container traffic at about 57,000 TEU in 2024, against about 18 million TEU of seaborne container trade from Asia to Europe ([RVO](https://www.rvo.nl/sites/default/files/2025-12/MarketStudyPorts%20in%20Romania%20and%20Bulgaria%20-%20Final%20version.pdf)). The corridor carries well under 1% of that trade. It is a useful option, not a replacement for the sea route.

Figures differ between sources and are often published by governments promoting the route. Treat them as indicative.

## Where it slows down

The corridor's weakness is that it is a chain of separate legs, each with its own operator, gauge, port and customs regime:

- **Caspian crossing:** limited vessel capacity and port handling at Aktau, Kuryk and Baku.
- **Rail gauge changes** between Chinese and former Soviet networks, and again entering Turkey or the EU.
- **Multiple customs borders**, each adding paperwork and time.
- **Black Sea security:** war-risk insurance and shipping risk since 2022 add cost to any sea leg.
- **Coordination:** no single operator controls the route end to end, so delays at one leg ripple through.

Promoters often cite transit times well below the sea route; real-world times depend heavily on how these handovers go. Ask for a carrier's actual recent times, not the brochure.

## Bulgaria's position

Bulgaria's ports are smaller than Romania's and face strong competition:

| Port | Throughput 2024 | Containers | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Constanța (Romania) | about 58 Mt (about 67 Mt in 2023) | DP World terminal designed for about 1.5m TEU a year | The region's main gateway; ferry to Poti in Georgia |
| Burgas | about 19 Mt | roughly 200,000 TEU; new terminal opened 2025 designed for about 350,000 TEU | Ferry to Batumi in Georgia; inland links described as modest |
| Varna | about 11 Mt | about 150,000–200,000 TEU | Main Bulgarian container gateway; rail-ferry terminal at Beloslav used intermittently |

Source: [RVO market study, 2025](https://www.rvo.nl/sites/default/files/2025-12/MarketStudyPorts%20in%20Romania%20and%20Bulgaria%20-%20Final%20version.pdf).

The same study notes limited inland connectivity at both Bulgarian ports, and that much of Burgas's expansion is designed and permitted but waiting for commercial clients. Varna faces uncertainty from a possible concession tender. In July 2024 Bulgarian and Georgian railways agreed to restore a rail-ferry link between Varna and Georgia, but no restart date was given ([Railmarket](https://railmarket.com/news/freight-rail/23021-railway-ferry-link-between-bulgaria-and-georgia-to-be-revived)). We have not confirmed whether regular service has resumed.

Turkey is also a competitor. Cargo from Georgia can travel overland through Turkey and enter the EU at the Kapitan Andreevo crossing, bypassing Bulgarian ports altogether.

## Where the opportunity is real

- **Niche and time-sensitive cargo** between Central Asia, the Caucasus and south-east Europe, where the sea route via Suez is long and the route through Russia is closed.
- **Logistics services in Bulgaria**: warehousing, consolidation and customs near Varna and Burgas, if rail links improve.
- **Trade with Central Asia and the Caucasus themselves**, which are growing markets, not just transit points.

## Where the risk sits

The corridor's countries are also where Western enforcement agencies have most often found goods diverted to Russia since 2022. A route designed to avoid Russia can still be used to reach it. Buyers and sellers using it need end-user checks that match the risk.

## What this means for buyers

- What transit time has your carrier actually achieved in the last six months, not in its brochure?
- Who is responsible for the cargo at each handover, and who carries the risk of delay or loss?
- Does war-risk insurance cover the Black Sea leg, and at what cost?
- Is the corridor cheaper or faster than sea for your specific load, or only more resilient?
- For goods sold into the Caucasus or Central Asia, do you know the final user?

We do not give legal advice. Insurance, customs and sanctions questions belong with specialists.
