# CBAM for south-east European supply chains

Summary: Since 1 January 2026, importers bringing steel, aluminium, cement, fertilisers, electricity or hydrogen into the EU face a carbon charge at the border. For buyers sourcing these goods from Turkey, Serbia, North Macedonia or further east through Bulgaria, the Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanism is now a cost line, not a reporting exercise.
Published: October 2026
Date: 2026-10-25
Category: regulatory

## What CBAM does

The EU charges its own heavy industry for carbon emissions through the Emissions Trading System. CBAM applies an equivalent charge to the emissions embedded in certain imported goods, so that buying from outside the EU does not avoid the carbon cost. A transitional phase from October 2023 required reporting only. The definitive phase began on 1 January 2026, when importers became liable to buy and surrender CBAM certificates.

## What changed in the 2025 simplification

An amending regulation, (EU) 2025/2083, entered into force on 20 October 2025 and softened the rules for smaller importers ([Reed Smith](https://www.reedsmith.com/our-insights/blogs/viewpoints/102lr9t/what-you-need-to-know-as-cbam-simplification-comes-into-effect/)):

- **A 50-tonne annual threshold.** Importers whose total imports of CBAM goods stay at or below 50 tonnes a year are exempt. If an importer crosses 50 tonnes at any point, all of that year's imports become subject to CBAM. Electricity and hydrogen are excluded from the exemption.
- **Authorised declarant status.** Importers expecting to exceed the threshold must apply before they do so. Imports in scope that are not declared by an authorised declarant are refused customs clearance.
- **Later deadlines.** Certificate sales start in February 2027 for 2026 imports, and the annual declaration and surrender deadline moved to 30 September of the following year. For 2026 imports, that implies a first surrender in September 2027.
- **Lower holding requirement.** Importers must hold certificates covering 50% of embedded emissions each quarter, down from 80%.
- **Penalties** mirror the EU ETS rate of €100 per tonne of CO₂, and can reach five times that for importers who exceed the threshold without authorisation.

## Default values versus actual data

An importer can declare the actual emissions of the goods, verified by an accredited verifier, or use the Commission's default values. Defaults are set conservatively, based on the average of the highest-emitting exporters in a country, adjusted regionally, so they usually cost more than real data from an efficient plant ([Reed Smith](https://www.reedsmith.com/our-insights/blogs/viewpoints/102lr9t/what-you-need-to-know-as-cbam-simplification-comes-into-effect/)).

This is where the supplier relationship matters. A supplier that can provide installation-level emissions data, and have it verified, can save its EU customer money. One that cannot is pushing cost onto the buyer.

## Turkey: the largest regional exposure

The EU imported €10.83 billion of CBAM-covered goods from Turkey in 2025, according to Eurostat data cited by CBAM Guide ([CBAM Guide](https://cbamguide.com/news/2026-08-27-turkey-ets-implementing-regulation-cbam-article-9/)). Much of it is steel, and much of that moves by road through Bulgaria or by sea.

CBAM allows a deduction for a carbon price already paid in the country of production. Turkey published an emissions trading system regulation that took effect on 27 August 2026, but it creates no payment obligation yet and sets no allowance price; the pilot design is left to a future board decision. Under the Commission's draft rules, only a carbon price effectively paid can be deducted, and free allocation counts against it. As of late September 2026 the draft had not been adopted, so Turkish exporters' deduction remains zero ([CBAM Guide](https://cbamguide.com/news/2026-08-27-turkey-ets-implementing-regulation-cbam-article-9/)).

## The Western Balkans and Bulgaria

Serbia and North Macedonia export steel, aluminium and electricity to the EU and are outside the ETS, so their goods carry CBAM liability on entry. Bulgarian companies are on the other side: as EU businesses they pay ETS costs at home and, where they import CBAM goods from neighbours, act as the CBAM importer.

Two practical consequences for the region:

- **Price comparisons change.** A Turkish or Serbian steel quote that undercut an EU mill in 2025 now needs the CBAM cost added before comparison.
- **Data becomes a selling point.** Regional suppliers that measure and verify their emissions can compete against defaults; those that cannot will be priced as the worst in their country.

## What can go wrong

- **Not knowing you are the importer.** The CBAM obligation sits with the importer of record. If you buy on delivered-duty-paid terms, check who that is.
- **Crossing 50 tonnes unexpectedly.** A few extra shipments late in the year can bring the whole year into scope, without authorisation in place.
- **Wrong classification.** Coverage is defined by customs codes, including some downstream products such as screws, bolts and certain steel articles.
- **Unverifiable supplier data.** Actual-emissions claims need accredited verification; unverified figures cannot be used.
- **Origin games.** Routing goods through a third country does not change where they were produced. Misdeclaring origin to reduce CBAM is customs fraud.

## What this means for buyers

- Do any goods you import fall under CBAM codes, and who is the importer of record?
- Will your annual CBAM imports exceed 50 tonnes, and do you have, or need, authorised declarant status?
- Can your suppliers provide verified installation-level emissions data, or will you rely on defaults?
- Have you priced the CBAM cost into supplier comparisons for 2026 and 2027?
- If a supplier's country introduces a carbon price, can it prove the price was actually paid?
