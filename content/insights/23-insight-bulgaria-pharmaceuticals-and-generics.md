# Pharmaceuticals and generics in Bulgaria: exports, Russia and supply-chain integrity

Summary: Bulgaria has a long-established generics industry, a large wholesale trade and a fast-growing clinical-trials business. Its export profile still carries a heavy Russian weighting, and its wholesale sector re-exports medicines across the EU. For buyers and partners, the questions are product integrity, distribution routes and customers.

Date: 2026-10-25

## The industry in numbers

Germany's trade agency GTAI, drawing on IQVIA data, describes the sector as follows ([GTAI](https://www.gtai.de/de/trade/bulgarien-wirtschaft/pharmaindustrie)):

- **Production.** About 10 billion tablets and 164 million liquid doses a year, worth about €343 million, mostly generics. Domestic output covers roughly a quarter of national consumption.
- **Main producers.** Sopharma and Teva, plus animal-health and biotech producer Huvepharma.
- **Exports.** About €1.3 billion, boosted by wholesalers' parallel trade within the EU.
- **Destinations.** Russia took just over a fifth of exports, followed by Germany (12%), Romania (8%), the Netherlands (4.2%) and France (3.4%).
- **Investment.** Announced projects include a €500 million Angelini Pharma plant for epilepsy medicines and a planned €140 million Huvepharma vaccine plant.
- **Clinical trials.** International companies spend about €100 million a year on trials in Bulgaria, and a 2024 law cut approval times to 35 days.

## The Russia question

Medicines are exempt from EU sanctions on Russia on humanitarian grounds. Bulgarian producers can lawfully keep selling to Russian customers, and do. UN trade data reported by Trading Economics put Bulgaria's pharmaceutical exports to Russia at about US$265 million in 2025 ([Trading Economics](https://tradingeconomics.com/bulgaria/exports/russia/pharmaceutical-products)).

That creates three considerations for a Western partner:

- **Reputation.** Being a supplier, licensor or contract manufacturer to a company with large Russian sales may attract questions from your own stakeholders, even though it is legal.
- **Payment channels.** Russian payments run through a narrow set of banks. Ask how a partner is paid and whether any payment intermediary is sanctioned.
- **Inputs and equipment.** The humanitarian exemption covers medicines, not everything around them. Laboratory equipment, chemicals, packaging machinery or software going to Russia can still be restricted.

## Product integrity

The EU's Falsified Medicines Directive requires unique identifiers and tamper-evident packaging on prescription medicines, with each pack verified at the point of dispensing. Manufacturers, importers and wholesalers need licences and must follow good manufacturing practice or good distribution practice, inspected in Bulgaria by the Bulgarian Drug Agency.

Bulgaria's large wholesale and parallel-trade sector moves medicines bought in Bulgaria, where prices are lower, to higher-priced EU markets. Parallel trade is legal within the EU, but long distribution chains create openings for diversion, theft and falsified products to enter the legitimate supply chain. Buyers should know every link.

Contract research is a growth area with its own risks: data integrity, patient consent and the reliability of trial sites. Sponsors should audit sites rather than rely on intermediaries.

## Who the buyers are

- **Pharmaceutical companies** licensing products to, or sourcing from, Bulgarian manufacturers.
- **Wholesalers and parallel importers** buying from Bulgarian distributors.
- **Sponsors and contract research organisations** placing trials in Bulgaria.
- **Investors** in manufacturing, distribution or pharmacy chains.

## What to check

- Manufacturing or wholesale licence, and the latest inspection outcome from the Bulgarian Drug Agency.
- Good manufacturing or distribution practice certificates, verified in the EU's EudraGMDP database, not from a PDF.
- Share of sales to Russia, Belarus and other non-EU markets, and how payments are received.
- For wholesalers: who they buy from, who they sell to, and whether volumes match their licence and storage.
- Ownership and any links to designated persons.

## What this means for buyers

- Do you know where your Bulgarian partner's products go after they leave your contract?
- Are you comfortable with its Russian sales, and how they are paid?
- Have you verified licences and certificates in official EU databases?
- For trials, have you audited the sites, not just the intermediary?
