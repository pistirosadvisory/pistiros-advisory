# The EU deforestation rules and Balkan timber and furniture

Summary: The EU Deforestation Regulation will require companies selling wood products in the EU to prove where the wood came from and that it was harvested legally. Bulgaria is classed as low risk for deforestation, but its record on illegal logging means buyers should not treat "low risk" as "no checks".
Published: October 2026
Date: 2026-10-25
Category: regulatory

## The timetable, after two delays

The regulation was adopted in 2023 and has been postponed twice. Under the latest amendment, Regulation (EU) 2025/2650, published on 23 December 2025, it applies from 30 December 2026 to medium and large operators and traders, and from 30 June 2027 to micro and small operators ([Stibbe](https://www.stibbe.com/publications-and-insights/the-amended-eudr-what-has-changed-and-what-has-remained)).

The Commission's simplification review, published on 4 May 2026, did not reopen the regulation. It proposed further changes through guidance, a draft delegated act adjusting the product list, and an updated information-system act ([JD Supra](https://www.jdsupra.com/legalnews/the-eu-commission-publishes-its-report-4358936/)). Dates have moved before. Plan for December 2026 but watch for change.

## What it covers

Wood is one of seven commodities in scope, alongside cattle, cocoa, coffee, palm oil, rubber and soy. For wood, the product list includes logs, sawn timber, panels, pulp and paper, and furniture. Printed products such as books and newspapers were removed by the 2025 amendment ([Stibbe](https://www.stibbe.com/publications-and-insights/the-amended-eudr-what-has-changed-and-what-has-remained)).

The rules apply to wood harvested inside the EU as well as imported wood. A Bulgarian sawmill selling to a German furniture maker is in scope.

## What operators must do

A product can be placed on or exported from the EU market only if it is deforestation-free, produced in line with the laws of the country of harvest, and covered by a due-diligence statement. Medium and large operators that first place products on the market must still carry out full due diligence: gather information, including the geolocation of the plots where the wood was harvested; assess the risk; and reduce it to negligible.

The 2025 amendment created a lighter category of "downstream operators", such as a furniture maker using timber already covered by a due-diligence declaration. They no longer need to repeat full due diligence but must keep supplier and customer records for five years and act on concrete evidence of non-compliance ([Stibbe](https://www.stibbe.com/publications-and-insights/the-amended-eudr-what-has-changed-and-what-has-remained)).

## Country risk: why "low risk" is not "no risk"

In May 2025 the Commission classified 140 countries as low risk, including every EU member state, and four as high risk: Russia, Belarus, Myanmar and North Korea. All others are standard risk ([Fieldfisher](https://www.fieldfisher.com/en/insights/the-eu-deforestation-regulation-european-commission-classifies-countries-as-high-low-or-standard-risk)).

Low-risk status allows simplified due diligence: operators skip the risk-assessment and mitigation steps unless credible information points to non-compliance. They still have to collect information, including plot locations.

For Bulgaria, the deforestation question and the legality question are different. Bulgaria's forest area is not shrinking in the way the regulation targets. Illegal logging is the problem:

- The Center for the Study of Democracy estimates illegal harvesting averaged 25–28% in 2013–2017, falling to 15–16% of demand in 2023, with a WWF estimate of 20%. It describes overcutting by legal companies, rigged tenders, forged stamps and front companies, and notes that illicit wood can exceed 30–40% of what some local mills process ([Center for the Study of Democracy, 2024](https://forest-trends.org/wp-content/uploads/2022/01/Bulgaria_Illegal-Logging_Publication_V2-2024.pdf)).
- Investigative journalists at BIRD compiled official transport tickets and estimated that around a tenth of the annual harvest of 6–7 million cubic metres was cut above permitted limits ([BIRD, 2020](https://bird.bg/en/gori-gate/)). This is a 2020 estimate and a journalists' calculation, not an official figure.

Bulgaria has introduced GPS tracking of timber trucks, cameras and electronic logbooks at depots, and electronic transport tickets. The same research describes these being gamed, for example one ticket reused for several trucks or trackers switched off ([Center for the Study of Democracy, 2024](https://forest-trends.org/wp-content/uploads/2022/01/Bulgaria_Illegal-Logging_Publication_V2-2024.pdf)).

"Credible information suggesting non-compliance" is exactly what removes the benefit of simplified due diligence. For Bulgarian wood, that information exists in the public domain.

## Neighbours

Wood from Serbia, North Macedonia, Turkey or Ukraine entering the EU through Bulgaria needs its own country classification checked. Wood with any origin in Russia or Belarus is high risk and, in most cases, also sanctioned.

## What this means for buyers

- Are you an operator, a downstream operator or a trader under the amended rules, and which deadline applies to you?
- Can your Bulgarian supplier give plot-level geolocation for the wood it sells you, not just a region?
- Does it buy from state forest enterprises with documented tenders, or from intermediaries and small loggers?
- Can it show electronic transport tickets matching the volumes it ships?
- Does the volume a mill sells match what its permits and purchases could produce?
- If any wood originates outside the EU, which country, and what is its risk class?

We do not give legal advice. Classification and due-diligence obligations belong with counsel.
