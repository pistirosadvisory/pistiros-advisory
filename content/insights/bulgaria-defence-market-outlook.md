# Bulgaria's defence industry: history, today, and outlook

Published: October 2026
Date: 2026-10-17
Category: sectors

Summary: Bulgaria has a long-established ammunition and small-arms industry that is now attracting Western investment and EU money, but buyers need to price a history of re-export, state-owned governance and patchy ownership data.


## A short history

Under communism, arms were one of Bulgaria's main industries. Human Rights Watch, citing earlier estimates, reports that the sector employed more than 100,000 people in 1989 and that most of its output was exported. Arms sales may have been as much as 9 percent of export earnings in the 1980s. Official figures were never published, so every number from this period is an estimate. The state trading firm Kintex was the only authorised exporter and, according to Human Rights Watch, was closely linked to the security services of the time.

After 1989 the markets collapsed. Some customers did not pay, UN embargoes blocked orders, and by 1992 unsold stock was estimated at over US$800 million. Some plants closed or paused production.

The same period left a reputation problem. Reference sources describe surplus and newly made weapons being exported illicitly to groups in Africa and Asia in the 1990s. Bulgaria later tightened its controls, including closer authentication of end-user certificates, but buyers still ask about that history.

What survived is a base of plants that make Soviet-calibre ammunition and weapons. That is the reason the industry mattered again after 2022.

## The industry today

Public information on ownership is uneven. The list below describes what each company makes, as reported in public sources; it says nothing about any company's conduct.

- VMZ Sopot: described by Euro-SD (2023) as the only state-owned arms plant in Bulgaria. It makes mostly Soviet-era ammunition, including 122 mm rockets, tank and artillery rounds, mortar bombs and anti-tank guided missiles. The wording "only" sits uneasily with TEREM's state ownership below, so check the current position.
- Arsenal (Kazanlak): described as a private joint-stock company, founded in 1878. It makes small arms and a wide range of ammunition, including 23 mm and 30 mm rounds.
- Dunarit (Ruse): makes rockets, free-fall bombs, mortar bombs and tank and artillery rounds. Ownership was not stated in the sources we found.
- TEREM: a state-owned Ministry of Defence company focused on repair, modernisation and ammunition maintenance. Its Kostenets plant is a subsidiary of the state-owned TEREM-Holding.
- Kintex: the state-owned defence trading company, historically Bulgaria's main authorised arms exporter.
- Samel 90 and Optix: privately held makers of military electronics and of thermal and night-vision optics respectively.

The largest new project is a joint venture between Rheinmetall and VMZ Sopot, signed on 28 October 2025. The government states a total investment of about EUR 1 billion for a plant producing gunpowder, 155 mm shells and modular charges, with nearly 1,000 jobs. Press coverage reports a 51 percent Rheinmetall and 49 percent VMZ split and annual output targets. Neither appears in the official release, so treat them as unconfirmed. One outlet reported the investment as close to one billion leva, which is roughly half the euro figure, so the headline amount also needs checking.

The project has since stalled. In July 2026 the new economy minister, Alexander Pulev, said: "There is no founding agreement. There is no joint venture between VMZ-Sopot and Rheinmetall." He added that Bulgaria's contribution had not been funded and the proposed site lacked infrastructure, and talks are restarting from scratch. The former prime minister says a shareholder agreement was signed in October 2025. Rheinmetall says its objectives are unchanged ([bne IntelliNews](https://www.intellinews.com/bulgaria-rheinmetall-to-restart-talks-on-planned-munitions-plant-456631/); [Capital](https://www.capital.bg/en/business/2026/07/17/4936543_will_the_rheinmetall_deal_fall_through/)). For buyers, the point is that the legal status of Bulgaria's largest defence investment depends on who is in government.

## What is driving demand

Exports rose quickly. The Ministry of Economy reports total defence-related exports and transfers of EUR 1.65 billion in 2022, EUR 2.17 billion in 2023 and EUR 2.83 billion in 2024, a record.

Ukraine is part of that story, but not a simple one. Direct contracts with Ukraine were zero in 2022, EUR 12.6 million in 2023 and EUR 156 million in 2024. Euractiv-based reporting estimates at least US$1 billion reached Ukraine indirectly in 2022, mainly via sales to other countries. The ministry says it cannot tell how much of what was sold elsewhere was later passed on. Reuters reported in June 2026 that the new defence minister said Bulgaria would send no more weapons to Ukraine.

Other drivers:

- EU SAFE: Bulgaria's loan is about EUR 3.3 billion. A first payment of EUR 489.3 million, 15 percent of the total, arrived on 28 August 2026. SAFE favours procurement of EU-made ammunition, missiles and air defence.
- NATO and national spending: Defence minister Stoyanov put 2025 spending at about 2.1 percent of GDP. The government's plan aims for 5 percent by 2035, of which 3.5 percent is core military spending.

## Potential and risks

The potential is real: existing plants, trained workforce, Western partners and EU financing. A buyer sourcing 122 mm, 152 mm or 30 mm ammunition, or an investor looking at a supply chain, has more options than a decade ago.

The risks should be priced rather than assumed away.

- Diversion and re-export: official data cannot trace goods sold to intermediaries. The Ukraine routing in 2022 shows how that gap works.
- End-use and sanctions checks: end-user certificates have a history of forgery. Screening has to cover the final recipient, not just the first buyer.
- State-owned governance: the biggest producers are state-linked, so board appointments, procurement and pricing can shift with politics. The Rheinmetall reversal above is a live example.
- Licensing: exports need an activity licence and a transfer permit under Bulgarian law, which follows the EU Common Position on arms exports. Dual-use goods fall under EU Regulation 2021/821. We have not checked the current consolidated text of the Bulgarian law.
- Ownership opacity: as the list above shows, public sources often leave ownership unstated.

## What this means for buyers

- Who ultimately owns and controls the supplier, and can you see that in a registry rather than a brochure?
- Is the supplier a producer, or a trader sitting between a producer and the buyer?
- Which licences does it hold, and does the permit cover your specific item, quantity and destination?
- What end-use documents will you accept, and how are they checked with the issuing authority?
- Can the supplier show where its goods went in the last three years, and who else touched them?
- If the supplier is state-linked, who can change its management or pricing, and what happens to your contract if they do?

We do not give legal advice. Licence and classification questions belong with independent counsel.
