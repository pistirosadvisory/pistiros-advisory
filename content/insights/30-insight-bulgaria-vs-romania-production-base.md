# Bulgaria vs Romania as a production base

Summary: Bulgaria and Romania joined the EU together in 2007, share the Danube border and compete for the same nearshoring investment. Romania offers scale; Bulgaria offers lower taxes, the euro and a tidier public balance sheet. The right choice depends on what you need most.

## Where Romania wins

- **Scale.** Three times the population means a larger labour pool and domestic market. Romania's stock of foreign investment was about €125 billion at the end of 2024, with manufacturing the largest share at 28%, led by transport equipment ([JOI/JETRO](https://www.joi.or.jp/wp-content/uploads/2025/12/20251209_ROU.pdf)).
- **Automotive depth.** Dacia, Ford Otosan and large tier-one suppliers such as Continental and Bosch anchor an established vehicle cluster.
- **Incentives.** Romania offers a 0% tax on reinvested profit in new technological equipment and up to ten years of tax exemption for companies doing only research and development ([JOI/JETRO](https://www.joi.or.jp/wp-content/uploads/2025/12/20251209_ROU.pdf)).
- **Black Sea port.** Constanța is far larger than Varna and Burgas combined.

## Where Bulgaria wins

- **Tax.** A 10% corporate rate and 5% dividend tax, against Romania's 16% on both. For large multinationals the EU's 15% global minimum tax narrows the gap; for small and mid-sized firms it does not.
- **Currency.** Bulgaria has adopted the euro; Romania has not and has no near-term date, given its deficit.
- **Fiscal stability.** Romania's deficit has been among the highest in the EU, prompting tax rises in 2025 on VAT, dividends, bank revenues and excise. Further tax changes are possible while it consolidates. Bulgaria's deficit is rising too, but from a much lower base.
- **Labour cost.** Bulgaria's minimum wage is now below Romania's.
- **Defence base.** Bulgaria has a larger legacy ammunition and small-arms industry.

## Shared weaknesses

Both countries face shrinking working-age populations, emigration of skilled workers, uneven rule of law and infrastructure gaps along the Danube, where only a handful of bridges connect them. Both have weaker anti-corruption records than Western Europe. Supplier checks are needed in either.

## Using both

Many companies treat the two as one region. The Danube crossings at Ruse–Giurgiu and Vidin–Calafat allow a plant in northern Bulgaria to serve customers in Bucharest, and a Romanian supplier to feed a Bulgarian assembler. Schengen removed routine border checks between them in 2025, and Bulgaria's euro adoption means only one side now carries currency risk.

## What this means for buyers and investors

- Do you need scale and an existing automotive cluster, or lower tax and the euro?
- How exposed are you to further Romanian tax changes while it reduces its deficit?
- Is labour availability, not cost, your binding constraint, and in which region of either country?
- Could a cross-Danube set-up give you the benefits of both?
