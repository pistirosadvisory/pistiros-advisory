# State-owned companies in defence and energy: how to read governance risk

Summary: Many of Bulgaria's largest counterparties in energy, transport and defence are owned by the state. They are often solid businesses with real assets. Their management, pricing and commitments can also change when the government does. Buyers and investors should price that.
Published: October 2026
Date: 2026-10-25
Category: risk

## The scale of the state sector

Bulgaria's Public Enterprises and Control Agency reports 266 public enterprises under dominant state influence at the end of 2023, 50 of them classed as large. Together they held BGN 57.9 billion of assets, earned BGN 22.1 billion of operating revenue and employed about 121,000 people. Most were profitable, but 73 posted combined losses of about BGN 437 million and 23 had negative equity ([Public Enterprises and Control Agency](https://appk.government.bg/upload/10030/Annual+summary+report+2023.pdf)).

Energy dominates by size: the state electricity, gas and coal companies under the Bulgarian Energy Holding, plus the Kozloduy nuclear plant. In defence, the state owns VMZ Sopot and the Ministry of Defence's TEREM group, alongside private producers such as Arsenal.

## The governance framework

Bulgaria adopted a Public Enterprises Act in 2019 and a central agency to monitor state companies, in part to meet OECD and EU expectations. An OECD review published that year identified the main challenges as stronger coordination of ownership, more independent and qualified boards, and better transparency and disclosure ([OECD](https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/oecd-review-of-the-corporate-governance-of-state-owned-enterprises-bulgaria_e64274bf-en.html)). The European Commission has funded further work on the framework through its reform support programme.

The law provides for competitive selection of board members. In practice, management turnover still tracks elections.

## What happened in 2026

The government that took office in May 2026 moved quickly:

- On 21 May 2026 the energy minister replaced the board of the Bulgarian Energy Holding, citing the need for tighter financial oversight. She said an inspection had found delays in reforms and weaknesses in investment programmes that put more than €1 billion of EU recovery and just-transition funding at risk, and announced audits of the holding, the Kozloduy new-build company and the Maritsa East mines ([BNR](https://bnrnews.bg/en/post/473447/bulgaria-replaces-management-of-state-energy-holding-beh)).
- By early July, according to Capital as summarised by BTA, the government had begun replacing management at other major state companies, including the water holding and the motorways company ([BTA](https://www.bta.bg/en/news/bulgaria/1161756-media-review-july-6)).
- In defence, the new economy minister said in July that no founding agreement or joint venture existed between VMZ Sopot and Rheinmetall, reversing the previous government's position on Bulgaria's largest defence investment. This is covered in our [defence-industry note](insight-bulgaria-defence-market-outlook.html).

None of this is unusual for Bulgaria. Every recent change of government has brought management changes in state companies. The point for a counterparty is that it should be expected.

## How governance risk shows up

- **Decisions stall** before and after elections, as managers wait for direction.
- **Contracts are reopened.** New management may review commitments made by predecessors, as with the Rheinmetall project.
- **Procurement is re-run** or delayed when leadership changes.
- **Pricing can be political.** Regulated or state-set prices respond to social pressures as well as costs.
- **Audits and investigations** follow changes of government and can tie up counterparties.
- **Payment risk** rises where a state company has negative equity or depends on state support.

## How to assess a state-owned counterparty

- **Read the filed accounts.** State companies file in the Commercial Register like any other. Check equity, debt, losses and dependence on subsidies.
- **Map the chain of control.** Which ministry exercises ownership, through which holding, and who sits on the board today.
- **Track management history.** How often has the board changed, and did changes follow elections?
- **Check the agency's assessment.** The Public Enterprises and Control Agency rates the business programmes of large state companies; in its 2023 report, 49 of the 50 large enterprises were rated good or very good and one unsatisfactory ([Public Enterprises and Control Agency](https://appk.government.bg/upload/10030/Annual+summary+report+2023.pdf)).
- **Look for open audits or investigations.**
- **Build continuity into contracts.** Governing law, arbitration, change-of-control and termination terms, and payment security, matter more when the counterparty's management can change overnight.

## What this means for buyers and investors

- Which ministry ultimately controls your counterparty, and how stable is that minister's position?
- Has the counterparty's board changed since your deal was agreed?
- Does your contract survive a change of management, and how would you enforce it?
- Is the counterparty financially self-sufficient, or dependent on state support?
- Are there audits or investigations open that could affect delivery or payment?
