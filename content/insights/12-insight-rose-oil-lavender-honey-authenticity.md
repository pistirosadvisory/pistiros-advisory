# Rose oil, lavender and honey: authenticity risk in Bulgarian agri-food

Summary: Bulgaria's best-known natural products, rose oil from the Kazanlak valley, lavender oil and honey, sell on origin and purity. That is exactly what makes them targets for adulteration and mislabelling. For buyers, verifying the supplier and verifying the product are two separate checks.
Published: October 2026
Date: 2026-10-25
Category: sectors

## Why these products attract fraud

The economics are simple: when the genuine product is scarce and expensive, and a cheaper substitute is hard to detect, dilution pays.

- **Rose oil.** Producing 1 kg of oil takes roughly 3,500 kg of rose petals, harvested by hand over a few weeks ([Premium Beauty News](https://www.premiumbeautynews.com/en/bulgaria-relies-on-geographical,8325)). Prices for Damask rose oil can reach about US$11,000 per kg, and Bulgaria and Turkey together supply up to 90% of world production, while meeting only about half of annual demand ([UCL](https://www.ucl.ac.uk/life-sciences/news/2024/dec/botanical-adulterants-prevention-bulletin-rose-rosa-damascena-essential-oil)).
- **Lavender oil.** Bulgaria has been reported as the world's largest producer; a 2015 report cited 170 tonnes of about 400 tonnes traded worldwide ([BNT](https://bntnews.bg/news/bulgaria-is-again-the-world’s-biggest-producer-of-lavender-oil-1315661news.html)). We have not found a more recent comparable figure. Prices are much lower than rose oil, but volumes are large and lavandin, a cheaper hybrid, is a common substitute.
- **Honey.** In an EU-wide action between November 2021 and February 2022, 147 of 320 imported honey consignments, 46%, were suspected of not complying with EU honey rules, mainly through dilution with sugar syrups. Rice-based syrups sell for about €0.40–0.60 per kg against an average imported honey value of €2.32 per kg in 2021 ([European Commission JRC](https://joint-research-centre.ec.europa.eu/jrc-news/food-fraud-how-genuine-your-honey-2023-03-23_en)). These were suspicions, not confirmed findings, and concerned imports into the EU, but they show how the economics work.

## Common forms of fraud

**Rose oil**

- Dilution with cheaper oils such as geranium or palmarosa, or with synthetic aroma chemicals ([UCL](https://www.ucl.ac.uk/life-sciences/news/2024/dec/botanical-adulterants-prevention-bulletin-rose-rosa-damascena-essential-oil)).
- Origin misrepresentation: oil from elsewhere sold as Bulgarian.
- Selling rose absolute (solvent-extracted) or rose water concentrates as distilled oil.

**Lavender oil**

- Substituting or blending lavandin for true lavender (Lavandula angustifolia).
- Adding synthetic linalyl acetate or linalool to meet specifications.

**Honey**

- Dilution with sugar syrups.
- Mislabelled floral or geographic origin, for example blended imported honey sold as Bulgarian.
- Harvesting immature honey and drying it artificially.

## Protections that exist

- **"Bulgarian rose oil" is a protected geographical indication**, on the EU register since October 2014 ([Premium Beauty News](https://www.premiumbeautynews.com/en/bulgaria-relies-on-geographical,8325)). Only oil meeting the specification and produced in the defined area may use the name.
- **A national authenticity laboratory.** In August 2026 the agriculture minister announced €500,000 for a specialised laboratory to distinguish natural from artificial rose oil, to be operational by the end of 2026. He said the state had not previously had that capacity, which created an opening for artificial products to be presented as Bulgarian ([BNR](https://bnrnews.bg/en/post/517511/specialized-laboratory-to-guarantee-the-authenticity-of-rose-oil)).
- **Testing.** Gas chromatography with mass spectrometry can identify the chemical markers that separate genuine rose oil from imitations ([UCL](https://www.ucl.ac.uk/life-sciences/news/2024/dec/botanical-adulterants-prevention-bulletin-rose-rosa-damascena-essential-oil)). Honey testing uses a range of methods, but no single test catches every form of syrup adulteration.

## How a buyer protects itself

- **Buy from the distiller or beekeeper where possible**, not from traders several steps removed. Visit at harvest if the volume justifies it.
- **Check capacity against volume.** A distillery's rose fields, petal purchases and still capacity limit how much genuine oil it can produce. A supplier offering more than its inputs allow is selling something else.
- **Test independently.** Commission your own analysis from an accredited laboratory, on samples you draw, not samples the supplier sends.
- **Ask for the paper trail**: PGI entitlement, harvest year, batch numbers, distillation records, and for honey, apiary registration and hive locations.
- **Watch the price.** Genuine Bulgarian rose oil cannot be sold far below the market. A bargain is a signal.
- **Write it into the contract**: specifications, test methods, the right to reject, and remedies for non-conforming batches.

## For brands selling to UK and EU consumers

A brand that labels a product as containing Bulgarian rose oil or Bulgarian honey is responsible for that claim. Cosmetic products in the UK and EU need a responsible person and a safety assessment, and the responsible person must be able to substantiate ingredient claims. If the supplier's oil turns out to be adulterated, the brand carries the regulatory and reputational damage.

## What this means for buyers

- Do you know who distilled or harvested the product, not just who sold it to you?
- Does the supplier's production capacity support the volume it offers?
- Have you tested a sample you drew yourself, with an accredited laboratory?
- Is the price consistent with genuine product for that harvest year?
- For rose oil labelled as Bulgarian, does the supplier hold the right to use the protected name?
- Does your contract define specifications, testing and remedies?
