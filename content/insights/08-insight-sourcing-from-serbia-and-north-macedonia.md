# Beyond the EU border: sourcing from Serbia and North Macedonia

Summary: Serbia and North Macedonia sit next to Bulgaria, share much of its industrial history and often quote lower prices. For a Western buyer, the difference is not geography but rules: a supplier one border west of Sofia operates outside the EU's legal framework, and in Serbia's case outside its sanctions regime.
Published: October 2026
Date: 2026-10-25
Category: regional

## Same region, different rules

Both countries are EU candidates with trade agreements that give most industrial goods duty-free access to the EU. That makes them look like part of the single market. They are not.

| | Bulgaria | North Macedonia | Serbia |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| EU member | Yes | Candidate | Candidate |
| Customs border with the EU | No | Yes | Yes |
| EU sanctions on Russia apply | Yes, directly | Aligned voluntarily | Not aligned |
| EU foreign-policy alignment (2024) | n/a | 100% | 51% |
| Corruption Perceptions Index 2025, score and rank (of 182) | 40, 84th | 40, 84th | 33, 116th |

Corruption index figures are from [BTA](https://www.bta.bg/en/news/1060672-bulgaria-s-score-in-corruption-perceptions-index-2025-is-country-s-worst-since-2) and [European Western Balkans](https://europeanwesternbalkans.com/2026/02/10/western-balkans-grapple-with-deepening-corruption-serbia-hits-regional-low/); Serbia's 2025 score is its lowest since the current method began in 2012. The alignment figures are from the European Parliament's 2025 report on Serbia, which records Serbia's alignment falling from 54% in 2023 to 51% in 2024, with North Macedonia at 100%, and notes that Serbia has not imposed sanctions on Russia ([European Parliament](https://www.europarl.europa.eu/doceo/document/A-10-2025-0072_EN.html)). North Macedonia joined the EU's Russia sanctions in February 2022 ([European Western Balkans](https://europeanwesternbalkans.com/2022/02/24/north-macedonia-joins-eus-package-of-sanctions-against-russia/)).

## What changes for a buyer

**Customs and origin.** Goods cross an external EU border, so they need export and import declarations and proof of preferential origin, usually an EUR.1 certificate or an origin declaration, to enter duty-free. If inputs come from outside the region, origin rules may deny the preference.

**Export controls in both directions.** Moving most dual-use items between EU member states needs no licence. Exporting them from the EU to Serbia or North Macedonia does. If your Bulgarian or Greek supplier ships controlled components to a sub-supplier in Serbia for assembly, that leg is an export.

**Sanctions exposure.** A Serbian supplier is not bound by EU sanctions. It may legally trade with Russian customers that an EU firm could not. That does not make it a bad supplier, but it changes what your checks must cover: its other customers, its owners, and where its inputs and outputs go.

**Product rules and standards.** EU product law (CE marking, chemicals rules, product safety) applies when goods enter the EU, and the importer carries responsibility. A candidate country's own certificates may follow EU standards without being EU-recognised.

**Courts and enforcement.** A contract dispute with a Serbian or Macedonian supplier is resolved under local law or arbitration, not in an EU court. Enforcing a judgment takes longer and costs more.

## Serbia in particular

Serbia has a larger industrial base than North Macedonia, including an established defence industry. The European Parliament's 2025 report raises concerns about judicial independence and political pressure, describes limited progress against corruption, and cites unresolved high-profile cases, including one involving the state ammunition maker Krušik ([European Parliament](https://www.europarl.europa.eu/doceo/document/A-10-2025-0072_EN.html)).

Its energy sector also carries Russian links: the same report notes US sanctions on the oil company NIS, a Gazprom subsidiary. Buyers should expect Serbian suppliers to have Russian customers, banks or shareholders more often than Bulgarian ones, and should check for them.

## North Macedonia in particular

North Macedonia's full alignment with EU foreign policy and sanctions makes it a lower-risk sourcing location on that dimension. Its industrial base is smaller and concentrated in automotive components, often in foreign-owned plants in free economic zones. Checks focus more on ownership, origin and quality than on sanctions.

## Why buyers still source here

The reasons are good ones: lower costs, available capacity, short trucking distances to EU plants, and engineering skills inherited from the Yugoslav industrial base. The aim is not to avoid these countries but to source from them with checks that fit the rules they actually operate under.

## What this means for buyers

- Is your supplier inside the EU, or one border away? Check the registered address, not the sales office.
- Do any of your controlled items cross into Serbia or North Macedonia for processing, and is that leg licensed?
- Does a Serbian supplier have Russian customers, owners, banks or inputs, and are you comfortable with each?
- Can the supplier prove preferential origin for every product it says enters the EU duty-free?
- Who in your organisation is the importer of record, and do you accept EU product-law responsibility for these goods?
- What law governs the contract, and where would you enforce it?
