Dual-use exports from Bulgaria: who licenses, how long it takes, and common mistakes

Many Bulgarian manufacturers make goods that have both civilian and military uses without thinking of themselves as exporters of controlled items. Sensors, optics, machine tools, electronics and certain chemicals can all need a licence to leave the EU. This note sets out how the Bulgarian system works and where companies usually go wrong.

October 2026 · This article is in English.

The legal frame

Dual-use goods in the EU are governed by Regulation (EU) 2021/821. Its Annex I lists controlled items, with technical thresholds; an item is controlled only if it meets them. The regulation applies directly in Bulgaria, and Bulgarian law adds the national procedures, the licensing authority and the penalties.

Two features catch companies out:

  • Catch-all controls. Even an item not on the list needs a licence if the authorities tell the exporter it is or may be intended for chemical, biological or nuclear weapons or their delivery missiles, for military end use in a country under an arms embargo, or as parts of military items exported illegally. An exporter who is aware of such a use must notify the authorities.
  • Technology and software count. The regulation defines export to include transmitting software or technology electronically outside the EU, so emailing a controlled design file can be an export (EUR-Lex).

Within the EU, most dual-use items move without a licence. A short list of the most sensitive items, in Annex IV, needs one even for transfers to another member state.

Who licenses in Bulgaria

The national law we reviewed, the Act on Export Control of Defence-Related Products and Dual-Use Items and Technologies, gives licensing to an Interdepartmental Commission for Export Control and Non-Proliferation under the Minister of Economy (Ministry of Economy, consolidated text dated 20.02.2015). The text we read predates the 2021 EU regulation and has since been amended, so check the current consolidated version before relying on any detail below.

Under that text:

  • Registration first. Exporters and transferors of dual-use items must register with the Ministry of Economy before trading. Registration lasts five years. Brokers of listed items must register too.
  • Types of authorisation. Exports can use an individual authorisation (one destination, valid up to one year), a global authorisation (a category of items, valid up to two years), a national general authorisation or an EU general authorisation. Transit can also need a case-by-case authorisation.
  • Timing. The Commission decides on individual and global applications within 30 days of receipt, can postpone for up to 60 days, and notifies the applicant within seven working days of deciding.
  • Records. Trade and transport documents must be kept for at least ten years.

EU general authorisations

The EU regulation includes Union general export authorisations for specific items and destinations; the best known, EU001, covers exports of most listed items to a group of close partner countries including the US, UK and Japan. Check the current annex for the exact list. They remove the need for an individual licence but carry conditions: registration before first use, reporting, and exclusions for the most sensitive items. Using one without meeting its conditions is the same as exporting without a licence.

Penalties

The 2015 text sets administrative fines, stated in leva before the euro changeover, for trading without the required registration or authorisation: BGN 25,000 to 250,000 for companies, BGN 1,000 to 50,000 for individuals and company officials, and higher amounts for repeat breaches. Breaching the terms of an authorisation or record-keeping rules carries separate fines. Serious cases can also be criminal. The amounts may have changed with later amendments.

For most companies the larger cost is not the fine but what follows: lost Western customers, bank relationships reviewed, and a record that appears in every future due-diligence check.

Common mistakes

  • Assuming civilian means uncontrolled. Classification depends on technical parameters, not on who the customer is.
  • Classifying from memory. Product specifications change; a new sensor, chip or software version can cross a threshold.
  • Forgetting technology. Drawings, source code and technical support sent abroad can be controlled exports.
  • Treating the distributor as the end user. The licence and the risk attach to the final use and user.
  • Ignoring catch-all. "It's not on the list" is not a defence once you have reason to suspect a sensitive end use.
  • Misusing general authorisations. Not registering, not reporting, or using one for an excluded item.
  • Poor records. If you cannot show what you exported, to whom and under which authorisation, you cannot show compliance.
  • No sanctions overlay. An export licence does not override sanctions. Russia-related rules, including the contractual no-re-export clause, apply on top.

A minimum programme for a small exporter

The European Commission's recommendation on internal compliance programmes describes seven core elements (EUR-Lex). For a small Bulgarian manufacturer, a workable minimum is:

  1. A named person responsible, with authority to stop a shipment.
  2. A product classification file, reviewed when products change.
  3. Customer and end-user screening against sanctions lists and red flags, recorded for each order.
  4. A rule for when a licence application is needed, and who files it.
  5. Training once a year for sales, engineering and shipping staff.
  6. Records kept for ten years.
  7. Controls on who can send technical files abroad.

What this means for buyers

  • Has your Bulgarian supplier classified the items it sells you, and can it show the classification?
  • Is it registered with the Ministry of Economy as a dual-use exporter if it needs to be?
  • Who is the exporter of record for shipments leaving the EU, you or the supplier?
  • Does the supplier have a written compliance programme, and who is responsible for it?
  • If you share technical drawings with the supplier, could any of them be controlled?
