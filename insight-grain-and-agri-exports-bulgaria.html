Grain and agri-exports through Varna and Burgas

Bulgaria is a significant exporter of wheat, sunflower and other crops, and its Black Sea ports also handle neighbours' grain. Since 2022 the trade has been shaped by Ukrainian exports, farmer protests and the risk of Russian-origin or stolen Ukrainian grain entering legitimate supply chains. Buyers need to know where each cargo really came from.

October 2026 · This article is in English.

The trade

According to Capital's analysis of 2024 trade data (Capital):

  • Wheat exports were just over €1 billion, down nearly a third on the previous year, with volume falling to just under 5 million tonnes from 6.4 million. Main buyers were Algeria, Spain and Egypt.
  • Sunflower seeds and oil fell sharply as production dropped by nearly 20%. Crude sunflower oil went mainly to Iraq, South Africa and Greece; refined oil mainly to Greece.
  • Smaller exports included unmilled rice, mainly to Turkey.

Output swings with weather. Drought hit southern and north-eastern Bulgaria in recent seasons, and the 2026 Gulf energy shock raised fertiliser and fuel costs. Prices and volumes are volatile year to year.

The Ukrainian grain dispute

After Russia's invasion, Ukrainian grain moved overland through EU neighbours. Cheap imports depressed local prices and Bulgaria, like Poland and Hungary, restricted imports of Ukrainian wheat, corn, rapeseed and sunflower seed in 2023. When parliament voted to let the ban lapse after 15 September 2023, farmers blocked roads and border crossings and demanded tighter control over the origin, quality and safety of imports (Ukrainska Pravda).

Bulgaria then moved to an import-licensing regime. In early 2024 the two agriculture ministers agreed to keep exchanging trade data, and Bulgaria said it would pass Ukraine the names of companies that had broken the licensing rules; under rules adopted in January 2024, such exporters lose their trusted-exporter status for six months (Interfax).

The episode matters because it showed that agricultural trade through Bulgaria is politically sensitive, and that licensing and origin rules can change quickly.

Origin risk: Russian and stolen grain

The bigger integrity risk is grain whose origin is disguised:

  • Russian grain is not banned from EU import, but EU tariffs on Russian and Belarusian cereals and oilseeds were raised sharply from 2024, creating an incentive to relabel origin.
  • Grain from occupied Ukraine. Ukraine's foreign intelligence service said Russia exported more than 2 million tonnes of grain grown on occupied Ukrainian territory in 2025, using ship-to-ship transfers near Kavkaz port and occupied Black Sea ports such as Sevastopol to hide origin. Named destinations included Egypt, Bangladesh, Turkey, Lebanon and Syria, plus smaller volumes to "some Balkan countries" (Euromaidan Press). These are claims by a party to the war; the published figures are also internally inconsistent.

For a buyer of grain or oilseeds loaded at a Black Sea port, including Bulgarian ports, the question is whether the documented origin is the real one.

What to check

  • Origin documents for every cargo: phytosanitary certificates, certificates of origin, and for EU-origin grain, farm and storage records.
  • Vessel history: AIS track, port calls and any ship-to-ship transfers before loading.
  • Seller substance: does a trading company that sells you 30,000 tonnes own storage, contracts with farmers or a history of trading, or was it formed recently?
  • Price: cargoes priced well below market for the season deserve questions.
  • Sanctions: Russian and Belarusian sellers, vessels and insurers can be sanctioned even where the grain itself is not.

Opportunities

  • Bulgaria's ports at Varna and Burgas compete for Black Sea grain handling, and grain terminal investment is under discussion.
  • Processing locally, such as sunflower crushing and flour milling, adds value compared with raw exports.
  • Traceable, certified EU-origin grain has a premium in some markets precisely because of origin risk elsewhere.

What this means for buyers

  • Can your seller prove origin to the farm or the silo, not just to the port?
  • Did the vessel make any unexplained stops or transfers before loading?
  • Is the seller a real trading business with assets and history?
  • Are you prepared for a sudden change in licensing or import rules?
