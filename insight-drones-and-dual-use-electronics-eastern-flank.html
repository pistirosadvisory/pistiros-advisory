Drones and dual-use electronics on the Eastern Flank

Europe wants drones and counter-drone systems quickly, and much of the hardware inside them still comes from China. Bulgaria has small but real drone makers and a long record in military electronics. For buyers, the questions are where the components come from, where the products go, and who controls the company.

October 2026 · This article is in English.

The demand

Since 2025 the EU has pushed for a layered "drone wall" along its eastern border to detect and stop hostile drones, alongside larger defence spending through the SAFE loan instrument. Ukraine's experience has made small, cheap drones and jamming equipment a priority for every European army. Bulgaria sits at the southern end of that border and on the Black Sea.

What Bulgaria makes

Public information on Bulgaria's drone sector is thin and mostly comes from company statements. Two examples:

  • Samel-90, Samokov. A privately owned military electronics maker. In October 2025 it told RFE/RL it builds the Samjet, a flying-wing one-way attack drone with an electric motor and a range of about 100 km, at about 50 a month, with capacity to triple. It also makes anti-drone jamming devices, which it said it exports, with Western European states not among its customers at the time (RFE/RL).
  • Dronamics. Best known for long-range cargo drones, it said in October 2025 that it was in talks with European and national bodies about contributing air-based surveillance to the drone wall. Its chief executive said all critical components are made in Europe, with carbon material from France and Austrian Rotax engines, and that it avoids reliance on US or Chinese suppliers (Novinite).

These claims come from the companies and have not been independently verified. Both are named as examples of the sector; nothing in this note suggests wrongdoing by either.

Government policy has shifted. In March 2026 the Ukrainian and Bulgarian defence ministers discussed joint drone and counter-drone production and testing in Ukraine (Government of Ukraine). After the April 2026 election, the new defence minister said in June that Bulgaria would send no more weapons to Ukraine, while commercial defence cooperation continues, as covered in our defence-industry note. Export opportunities for Bulgarian drone makers therefore depend on politics as much as product.

The component problem

Small drones are built from a short list of parts: brushless motors and the magnets inside them, batteries, flight controllers, cameras and radio links. China dominates supply of most of them.

  • In December 2024 Bloomberg reported that Chinese producers of motors, batteries and flight controllers were capping or halting shipments to Western customers, after China's 2023 restrictions on certain drones (Euromaidan Press).
  • In July 2026 the Financial Times reported, as relayed by WION, that Ukraine had been allowed in exceptional cases to use part of an EU defence loan to buy Chinese batteries, engines, flight controllers and electronics when European supply was not available in time (WION).

If Ukraine, with EU funding, still needs Chinese parts, a small Bulgarian maker is likely to as well. That creates three risks for a European buyer:

  • Supply risk. Beijing can restrict exports at short notice.
  • Programme eligibility. EU-funded procurement may require European content; a supplier's claims need checking against its bill of materials.
  • Security risk. Some buyers, especially for critical infrastructure and defence, will not accept Chinese flight controllers, radios or firmware.

The diversion problem

The same parts flow the other way. Drone components, cameras, radio modules and navigation electronics are among the goods Russia most wants and appear on the G7's common high-priority list. The Plovdiv case of 2023, where a company bought optical sights, ballistic computers and aviation radios and shipped them to Russia without a licence, shows that Bulgaria has been used as a route (mind.ua). A supplier of drone electronics needs export controls that match the sensitivity of what it sells.

What to check in a drone or electronics supplier

  • Ownership and control. Who owns it, and has that changed recently? Private ownership of former state plants is common and often lightly documented.
  • Bill of materials. Country of origin for motors, magnets, batteries, flight controllers, radios, cameras and firmware. Ask for it in writing.
  • Software and firmware. Who wrote it, where it is updated from, and whether it calls home.
  • Customers and end users. Where its drones and jammers have gone in the last three years, and how end use was checked.
  • Licences. Military and dual-use export licences held, and whether they cover your item and destination.
  • Capacity claims. Whether stated monthly output matches staff, floor space and component purchases.

What this means for buyers

  • Can the supplier give you a bill of materials with country of origin for every critical part?
  • Will your programme's European-content rules accept that bill of materials?
  • Who else does the supplier sell to, and would any of those customers be a problem for you?
  • Does the supplier's licensing cover the items and destinations you need?
  • If Chinese parts were cut off tomorrow, how long could the supplier keep producing?
