Red flags for re-export through the Balkans and the Black Sea

Bulgaria sits on the EU's land border with Turkey and on the Black Sea, which makes it a working route to the Caucasus and Central Asia. Since 2022 the same routes have been used to move controlled goods to Russia. Buyers and sellers who know the warning signs can price that risk instead of discovering it.

October 2026 · This article is in English.

Why this route matters

Diversion is rarely a smuggler crossing a border at night. It is usually a paper trail that looks normal: an EU seller, an intermediary in a third country, a final buyer nobody asked about.

Bulgaria has seen both ends of this. In October 2023 the State Agency for National Security and Plovdiv prosecutors detained 12 people, including three Russian citizens, over a scheme that bought dual-use goods through a Plovdiv-registered company, repackaged them in a residential building and exported them to Russia without a licence. Reported items included optical sights, ballistic computers and aviation radios, worth about US$2.17 million (mind.ua).

Two months later the then finance minister said the authorities had found a channel moving dual-use goods, including products with electronic components, in through the Bulgarian–Turkish border and on to Armenia, Azerbaijan and other countries, from where they were resold to Russia. The government announced 100% inspection of lorries on that route and closer tracking of transit patterns (BNT). We have not seen published results of those measures.

Neither case means a typical Bulgarian supplier is involved. Both show why a buyer cannot rely on the first link in the chain.

Which goods attract attention

The EU, UK, US and Japan maintain a Common High Priority List of items Russia has sought for its weapons. It covers 50 tariff lines, each identified by a six-digit Harmonised System code, split into tiers from integrated circuits (Tier 1) to CNC machine tools and parts (Tier 4.B) (Global Affairs Canada, G7 guidance).

Bulgaria's own export base overlaps with this list more than buyers expect. Its automotive and electronics suppliers make sensors, wiring and control electronics; its defence plants make ammunition and optics. Goods do not need to be on a military list to be high-risk.

The contract obligation many sellers miss

Since the EU's 12th sanctions package (December 2023), Article 12g of Regulation 833/2014 requires EU exporters selling certain goods to buyers outside the EU to include a clause prohibiting re-export to Russia or for use in Russia. It covers common high-priority items, aviation goods, jet fuel and firearms. It has applied to new contracts since 20 March 2024 and to older contracts still being performed since 20 December 2024. Buyers in partner countries, including the UK, the US, Norway, Switzerland and Japan, are exempt.

The clause must carry adequate remedies; the European Commission's guidance gives contract termination and a penalty as examples (Arnold & Porter).

For a Western buyer sourcing from Bulgaria, the practical question runs the other way too: does your Bulgarian supplier put this clause in its own sales to non-EU customers? If not, it is exposed, and so is its reputation as your supplier.

Red flags

The G7's 2024 industry guidance groups warning signs into a dozen headings (Global Affairs Canada). Applied to the Bulgarian and Black Sea routes, the ones we would prioritise are:

Customer and counterparty

  • A customer formed or restructured after February 2022, or whose ownership or management changed after a new sanctions package.
  • A customer whose business profile does not fit the goods: a food distributor ordering connectors, a consultancy ordering optics.
  • A customer that will not certify in writing that the goods will not go to Russia or Belarus.
  • Owners or directors who also appear in companies in Russia, Belarus, or known transit hubs.

Transaction

  • Payment from a third party not otherwise involved in the deal, especially from a different country.
  • Invoices split into smaller amounts that stay under licensing or reporting thresholds.
  • Last-minute changes to the consignee or delivery address, particularly from an EU or Turkish address to one in the Caucasus or Central Asia.
  • Prices well above market, or payment in advance without negotiation.

Logistics

  • A freight forwarder named as the end user.
  • Delivery to a warehouse, free zone or residential address rather than a production site.
  • Routing that makes no commercial sense, such as goods for a Turkish customer travelling via Georgia.
  • Repackaging or relabelling before onward shipment.

Documentation

  • End-user certificates that are generic, unsigned, or issued by someone other than the actual user.
  • Missing or inconsistent HS codes, or product descriptions that are vaguer on export than on the original order.

One flag alone rarely proves anything. Several together justify stopping the deal until they are explained.

Turkey, the Caucasus and Central Asia

The route through Bulgaria matters because of where it leads. Turkey is in a customs union with the EU for most industrial goods, which keeps border friction low. Beyond Turkey, the land and Black Sea routes reach Georgia, Armenia, Azerbaijan and, across the Caspian, Kazakhstan and the other Central Asian states. These are legitimate and growing markets. They are also where Western enforcement agencies have most often found diverted goods since 2022.

A buyer does not need to avoid these markets. It needs to know when its goods are heading there and who the final user is.

What this means for buyers

  • Does your Bulgarian supplier sell to customers outside the EU, and does it include a no-re-export-to-Russia clause where Article 12g requires one?
  • Can the supplier name the end user, not just the importer, for its non-EU sales of sensitive goods?
  • Who pays, from which country, and does that match the contract?
  • Has the supplier's customer list changed sharply since 2022?
  • If your own goods pass through Bulgaria in transit, who controls them while they are there?
