One year of the euro: what changed for Bulgarian suppliers

Bulgaria adopted the euro on 1 January 2026. For foreign buyers, most of the effect is good news: no exchange-rate risk, simpler pricing and payments. A few transition rules still matter, especially in contracts and company documents.

October 2026 · full-year update due January 2027 · This article is in English.

The basics

The conversion rate was fixed at 1.95583 leva to the euro, the same rate the currency board had used for decades. Leva and euro circulated together for one month, until 31 January 2026, after which businesses accept euro only. Bank accounts were converted automatically on 1 January 2026, free of charge and with unchanged IBANs (Ruskov & Kollegen).

Because the rate had been fixed for so long, the change removed little real currency risk. What it removed was friction: conversion costs, dual bookkeeping, and the remote possibility of a devaluation.

Prices: smaller effect than feared

The European Central Bank's analysis of the first months found that retail price changes at the changeover were minimal, typically a few euro cents, consistent with the legal rounding rules, with no systematic upward adjustment by large retailers. January 2026 did see unusual rises in some services, including restaurants, accommodation, rents, insurance and personal care; the ECB estimates inflation that month would have been 0.3–0.4 percentage points lower without them. By February only restaurants and accommodation and information and communication remained above their usual pattern (ECB).

Public support for the euro rose above 50% in January and reached 54% in February, the first majority in recent years, according to the same analysis.

Mandatory dual display of prices in leva and euro ran from 8 August 2025 and ended on 8 August 2026. National Revenue Agency data cited by Sofia Globe show 1,550 fines and about 700 penal decrees, totalling €1.8 million, issued under the euro introduction law over the whole period (Sofia Globe).

Contracts

The euro introduction law protects continuity. Existing contracts that refer to leva remain valid, and lev amounts are read as euro amounts at the official rate. The changeover does not excuse non-performance, and neither party may change or terminate a contract because of it (Ruskov & Kollegen).

Rounding follows fixed rules: divide by 1.95583 without shortening the rate, then round to two decimal places, with .005 and above rounded up. On large contracts with many line items, rounding each line versus rounding the total can produce small differences worth agreeing in advance.

Company records

  • Capital. The Registry Agency converted registered capital of limited and joint-stock companies to euro automatically on 1 January 2026, at no cost (CompanyData).
  • Articles of association. Companies still have to update their own founding documents. The original deadline was 12 months; CompanyData reports that parliament has extended it to 31 December 2028. Until then, older documents in a company's file may show capital in leva while the register shows euro. That is a timing difference, not a red flag.
  • Accounts. Financial statements for periods from 1 January 2026 are prepared in euro, with prior-year comparatives converted. When comparing a supplier's 2025 and 2026 accounts, check that you are reading both in the same currency.
  • Invoices and tax. Invoices have been issued in euro since 1 January 2026, and VAT returns and tax payments are made in euro. The VAT registration threshold was restated as €51,129.19 (Ruskov & Kollegen).

Banking and payments

Payments to Bulgarian suppliers are now domestic euro-area payments in the Single Euro Payments Area: faster and cheaper, with no conversion. For buyers outside the euro area, the currency exposure has simply moved from the lev to the euro.

Bulgaria's largest banks have been supervised by the ECB since the country entered close cooperation with euro-area banking supervision in October 2020, part of the path to the euro. That makes them more familiar counterparties for Western compliance teams.

What did not change

The euro did not change Bulgaria's courts, its public administration, its ownership-transparency rules or its corruption record. Euro membership lowers macroeconomic risk; it does not lower counterparty risk. Supplier checks stay the same.

What this means for buyers

  • Do your contracts with Bulgarian suppliers still state prices in leva, and have you agreed how rounding applies?
  • Are you comparing supplier accounts across 2025 and 2026 in the same currency?
  • Does a supplier's capital differ between its register record and its own documents? Usually a timing gap; ask anyway.
  • Have you moved payments to SEPA and removed conversion steps from your process?
  • Are you treating euro membership as a reason to relax counterparty checks? It is not one.
