Bulgaria on the Silk Road, then and now

Bulgaria was a corridor on routes that branched from the Silk Road, was largely bypassed by China's Belt and Road, and now sits at the EU end of the Middle Corridor. Each time, location brought traffic, but the rules of trade decided who profited.

October 2026 · This article is in English.

A crossroads, not a Silk Road capital

The lands of modern Bulgaria sit where the overland routes between the Mediterranean and Constantinople meet the Danube, the Black Sea and the steppe. That made them a place goods and armies passed through for 2,500 years.

The evidence does not support calling Bulgaria a main Silk Road node. The documented picture is a regional corridor: trade between the Aegean, Constantinople, the Danube and the Black Sea, with eastern goods reaching it only at the end of longer chains. Popular accounts often blur this. This article separates what is documented from what is not.

Part 1: Then

Thracian trade. Excavations at Pistiros, near Vetren in the Maritsa valley, show a river port and trading centre dated to roughly the fifth to third centuries BC, about 250 km from the Aegean coast (Liverpool). Scholars still debate how Greek or Thracian it was: recent work presents it as a Thracian city under Odrysian control rather than a colony (Balkan Ethnology journal).

Roman roads. The Via Militaris, also called the Via Diagonalis, linked Singidunum (Belgrade) to Constantinople through Naissus, Serdica (Sofia), Philippopolis (Plovdiv) and Adrianople (Wikipedia). Archaeologists have been excavating a roadside station, Bona Mansio, between Serdica and Philippopolis since 2002 (Archaeological Institute of America). Exact dates and lengths vary by source, so we give none here.

The medieval Bulgarian state. One trade dispute is unusually well recorded. In 894 Bulgarian merchants were moved from the Constantinople market to Thessaloniki, which led to a war between Symeon and Byzantium; some historians call it the first economic war in medieval Europe. What was traded is still hypothesis: one 2024 paper suggests textiles and barter (Raev, 2024). On the coast, finds in Nesebar (ancient Mesembria) show trade with Constantinople in the ninth to twelfth centuries (Nesebar Archaeological Museum). We found no solid trade figures for Pliska or Preslav, so we make no claim about them.

Volga Bulgaria is a different place. It was a separate state on the middle Volga, founded by a branch of the same Proto-Bulgarian people. It adopted Islam in 922, whereas the Danube state took Christianity in 864, and it traded furs and other goods with Central Asia and China (Bulgarian National Radio). Claims linking "Bulgarians" to the Silk Road usually mean this state, not the Balkan one.

Ottoman fairs. Under Ottoman rule the Bulgarian lands became a fair circuit. The Uzundzhovo fair, on the Plovdiv-to-Edirne road, ran regularly from the early eighteenth century to 1876 and drew up to 50,000 people over about 40 days (Wikipedia). Travellers in the sixteenth century described merchants from across the empire gathering at Plovdiv (Lost in Plovdiv). Sources we found do not tie Sofia or Ruse to Silk Road caravans, so we do not either.

Part 2: In between, the Belt and Road

China's Belt and Road Initiative, launched in 2013, was presented as a new Silk Road, and for a few years it looked as if the Balkans would be one of its gateways into Europe.

Bulgaria signed up early. It was one of the first EU governments to sign a Belt and Road memorandum, in November 2015, and Sofia hosted the summit of China and the Central and Eastern European states in July 2018 (Bulgarian Ministry of Foreign Affairs). In November 2025 the two governments marked ten years of the memorandum with a forum in Sofia and 17 new agreements (Belt and Road Portal, a Chinese government source).

The money went elsewhere. The flagship Belt and Road assets in south-east Europe are the Port of Piraeus in Greece, controlled by China's COSCO, which grew from about 0.9 million containers (TEU) in 2010 to several million a decade later (Hellenic Shipping News); Serbia's Smederevo steel plant and the Belgrade-Budapest railway; and Chinese battery and car plants in Hungary (CSIS). Bulgaria's best-known Chinese industrial project, the Great Wall car assembly plant near Lovech, stopped production in January 2016, and its operator filed for bankruptcy in 2017 (Sofia Globe).

In hindsight, being bypassed cost Bulgaria less than it seemed at the time. The EU has since moved from "connect" to "de-risk". Italy formally left the Belt and Road in December 2023, and Lithuania, Latvia and Estonia left the China-CEE format in 2021 and 2022 (Real Instituto Elcano; LRT). The Belgrade-Budapest line was still limited to freight in mid-2026 because its Chinese-supplied signalling lacked full certification (Railway News). Bulgaria arrives at the next corridor without large legacy commitments to unwind.

Part 3: Now, the Middle Corridor

The modern Middle Corridor (the Trans-Caspian route) links China, Kazakhstan, the Caspian Sea, Azerbaijan and Georgia to Europe. It has operated since 2017 (Times of Central Asia). Kazakhstan's transport ministry reports cargo of 2.76 million tonnes in 2023, 4.48 million in 2024 and 4.12 million in 2025, with a 2029 target of 300,000 containers (Astana Times). These are ministry figures, not independently verified, and volumes fell in 2025.

Bulgaria is a candidate exit, not yet part of the route. In 2025 Kazakhstan and Bulgaria said they would consider including Burgas and Varna (Times of Central Asia).

What has changed in Bulgaria's favour:

  • Schengen. Land border checks with Bulgaria and Romania ended on 1 January 2025 (eucrim). Truck inspections and customs at the external border are separate matters and did not change.
  • Euro. The Council approved adoption from 1 January 2026 at a fixed 1.95583 leva per euro (Council summary). This removes currency risk for euro-zone counterparties.
  • Danube links. Vidin to Calafat has had a second road-and-rail bridge since 2013 (BTA).
  • Energy, not only goods. Bulgaria announced in 2023 that it would join the Black Sea Green Energy Corridor, a planned subsea power cable to bring renewable electricity from Azerbaijan and Georgia to Romania and on to the EU. The project entered its development phase in 2026; seabed surveys are planned in Bulgaria's and Turkey's economic zones, and completion dates range from 2030 to the late 2030s depending on the source (Report.az; Interfax).

The limits are just as real:

  • Border capacity. At Kapitan Andreevo, on the Turkish border, a mid-December 2025 queue of trucks leaving Bulgaria reached about 27 km, with six to eight hours' wait. Capacity was 2,700 trucks a day, with expansion to 3,400 under way (BTA).
  • Rail. The transport minister has said about half of the rail network runs under speed restrictions (BNT; undated, so check currency).
  • Ports. A Dutch government study puts Burgas at about 19 million tonnes and Varna at about 11 million in 2024, and notes limited inland connections and competition from Constanta (RVO; figures as reported in secondary summary, not independently checked).
  • Sanctions diversion. In April 2026 the EU used its anti-circumvention tool for the first time, banning certain machine exports to Kyrgyzstan over suspected re-export to Russia (RFE/RL). Any route from Central Asia attracts this scrutiny.
  • Black Sea security. On 6 October 2026 drones struck two merchant ships in Bulgaria's exclusive economic zone; one sank. Bulgaria's prime minister said the attacker had not been identified (Euronews). Insurers will price this.

Read across the rows and one thing never changes: location gave Bulgaria traffic, but the money and the power sat with whoever wrote and enforced the rules of trade. What is new is that, for the first time, Bulgaria is inside the rule-setter rather than on its border. It is part of the EU's customs union, Schengen and the euro area. That is a stronger position than Bulgaria had on either the Silk Road or the Belt and Road.

Part 5: What carries over

A crossing point earns its value from trust and clear rules. The 894 dispute began with a change in where and on what terms foreign merchants could trade, and that is still how corridors win or lose business. The old toll has become compliance: ownership checks, sanctions screening and paperwork that stands up. A route that is fast but cannot show where goods came from and where they go will not keep Western buyers or insurers.

This is also why the corridor question is tied to whether Bulgaria can become a fast-growing "tiger" economy. Geography has been on Bulgaria's side for 2,500 years; what has been missing is the institutional trust that turns traffic into investment. We look at that gap in Is Bulgaria a Balkan Tiger?, and at the industries that would use the corridor in our notes on automotive and electronics and defence.
