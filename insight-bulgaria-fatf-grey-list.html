Bulgaria and the FATF grey list: what it means for counterparties

Since October 2023 Bulgaria has been on the Financial Action Task Force's list of jurisdictions under increased monitoring, usually called the grey list. A decision on removing it is expected at the FATF plenary ending on 30 October 2026. Whichever way that goes, the listing has shaped how banks and compliance teams treat Bulgarian counterparties, and the reasons for it still matter.

October 2026 · update due after the FATF plenary of 30 October 2026 · This article is in English.

What the grey list is

The FATF is the international standard-setter for anti-money-laundering and counter-terrorist-financing rules. Countries on its grey list have agreed an action plan to fix strategic weaknesses and are monitored until they complete it. It is not a sanctions list and does not ban business. In practice it leads banks and regulated firms to apply closer scrutiny, which means more questions, more documents and slower onboarding.

Why Bulgaria was listed

When Bulgaria was listed in October 2023 it committed to an action plan with ten areas, which together show where the weaknesses were (BTA):

  1. A comprehensive national action plan under its anti-money-laundering strategy.
  2. Closing remaining technical gaps in its laws.
  3. Risk-based supervision of certain sectors, and entry controls for virtual-asset service providers and postal money operators.
  4. Keeping beneficial-ownership information in the register accurate and current.
  5. An automated system to prioritise suspicious-transaction reports.
  6. More investigations and prosecutions of money laundering, including high-level corruption and organised crime.
  7. Pursuing confiscation of criminal assets as a policy goal.
  8. Parallel financial investigations in all terrorism cases.
  9. Closing gaps in targeted financial sanctions for terrorist and proliferation financing.
  10. Risk-based monitoring of non-profit organisations most exposed to terrorist-financing abuse.

Several of these bear directly on buyers: inaccurate ownership data, weak supervision of money transmitters and crypto firms, and limited prosecution of corruption-linked money laundering.

Where things stand

  • June 2026. The FATF concluded that Bulgaria had substantially completed its action plan and scheduled an on-site visit to confirm the reforms were working and irreversible (BTA).
  • September 2026. Bloomberg, cited by Bulgarian National Radio, reported that Bulgaria, Monaco and Côte d'Ivoire were expected to be removed at the FATF plenary on 30 October 2026. Removal requires consensus (BNR).

Check the plenary outcome before relying on either status.

What removal changes, and what it does not

Changes:

  • Banks and payment firms that applied enhanced checks because of the grey listing can step them back, so onboarding and payments should become smoother.
  • Bulgaria's reputation with correspondent banks and international investors improves.
  • The government loses an external reason to keep up the pace of reform, which is a risk in itself.

Does not change:

  • Bulgaria's Corruption Perceptions Index score of 40, 84th of 182 in 2025, its worst since 2012 and joint last in the EU with Hungary (BTA).
  • Individual counterparty risk. A clean country rating says nothing about a particular supplier's owners.
  • Your own obligations. Regulated firms still apply risk-based due diligence to every customer.

How the listing showed up in practice

During the listing period, foreign buyers and investors commonly saw:

  • banks asking more questions about payments to and from Bulgaria;
  • slower account opening for Bulgarian subsidiaries;
  • requests for source-of-funds evidence on Bulgarian investors;
  • closer review of Bulgarian payment institutions and crypto-asset firms.

If those frictions ease after removal, the underlying checks are still sensible for higher-risk sectors: defence, energy, fuel trading, construction and public contracts.

What this means for buyers

  • Has your bank or payment provider changed its treatment of Bulgarian counterparties, and will it change again after the plenary?
  • Do you rely on Bulgaria's beneficial-ownership register, given that its accuracy was one of the action-plan items?
  • If you pay through Bulgarian payment institutions or crypto-asset firms, are they licensed and supervised?
  • Will your internal country-risk rating change automatically on removal, and should it?
