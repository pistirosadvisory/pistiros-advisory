Georgia, Armenia and Azerbaijan: opportunity and re-export risk in the South Caucasus

The South Caucasus is where Bulgaria's Black Sea links lead east: ferries to Georgia, gas from Azerbaijan, and the Middle Corridor towards Central Asia. These are growing markets in their own right. Since 2022 they have also become the region where EU goods most visibly reappear in Russia. Both facts are true at once, and buyers need to handle both.

October 2026 · This article is in English.

Why the region matters to Bulgaria

  • Energy. Azerbaijani gas, delivered through Greece, has been one of Bulgaria's main supplies since Russian deliveries stopped in 2022.
  • Logistics. Burgas has a ferry service to Batumi and Varna a rail-ferry terminal used intermittently for Georgia; Bulgaria and Georgia agreed in 2024 to restore a rail-ferry link (Railmarket).
  • The Middle Corridor. Georgia and Azerbaijan are the land bridge between the Caspian and the Black Sea on the route that avoids Russia.

The re-export pattern

Since 2022, exports from the EU to some Caucasus and Central Asian states have jumped far beyond what local demand explains. Two well-documented examples:

Armenia. Polish Economic Institute analysis of Eurostat data shows EU exports to Armenia rising from about €0.76 billion in 2021 to €1.9–2.2 billion a year in 2022–2025. Goods on the EU's Russia export-ban lists, such as semiconductors, CNC machine tools and drones, rose from about €24 million to €91 million a month and accounted for 62% of the increase. In 2025, 48% of EU exports to Armenia were goods banned for export to Russia. Armenia's imports from Russia are dominated by gold that is quickly re-exported to the UAE and Hong Kong (PIE).

Georgia. Georgia re-exported about $1.2 billion of light vehicles in the first half of 2025, around 38% of its total exports, up almost fivefold since 2021. Kyrgyzstan and Kazakhstan were the declared destinations for 81%, but Kyrgyz import statistics fall well short of Georgian export figures, which researchers read as a sign that many cars end up in Russia. Georgia banned re-exports of certain EU cars to Russia and Belarus in 2023, and official figures show none since (Jamestown Foundation).

Neither government is legally obliged to apply EU sanctions. Re-exports to third countries are lawful under their laws. The obligation sits with the EU exporter, which must not supply goods it knows or should know are going to Russia, and must include a no-re-export-to-Russia clause for listed goods.

Azerbaijan

Azerbaijan is a different case: an energy supplier to Europe, a key Middle Corridor state, and not a known hub for re-exports to Russia on the scale of Armenia or Georgia. Risks there are more about governance, state-linked ownership and the long-running conflict with Armenia, which shapes routes and politics in the region. Note, though, that the EU's 20th sanctions package in April 2026 imposed transaction bans on several non-Russian financial institutions, with locations reported to include Azerbaijan as well as Kyrgyzstan (Baker McKenzie). Screen the banks your counterparties use.

The opportunity

Treated carefully, the region offers:

  • Real demand in construction, energy, logistics and consumer goods, as economies such as Armenia's grow quickly (7.2% in 2025, per PIE).
  • Transit services for the Middle Corridor, where Bulgarian ports and forwarders compete with Romania and Turkey.
  • Energy partnerships with Azerbaijan, linked to Bulgaria's gas security.

How to sell into the region without becoming a route

  • Classify your goods. If they appear on the EU's Russia export-ban lists or the G7 common high-priority list, treat sales to the region as high risk.
  • Know the end user. Ask for the name, address and business of the final user, not just the importer, and check it exists.
  • Match volumes to the market. An Armenian distributor ordering more chips or machine tools than the country plausibly uses is a warning.
  • Check payment routes. Third-party payers, payments from Russia or the UAE, and unusual currencies all need explaining.
  • Include the clause. The EU's no-re-export-to-Russia clause is mandatory for listed goods, with real remedies.
  • Monitor after sale. Watch for your products appearing on Russian marketplaces.

What this means for buyers and sellers

  • Do your sales to Georgia, Armenia or Azerbaijan include goods on EU Russia ban lists?
  • Can you name the final users, and do their order volumes make sense?
  • Does your Bulgarian supplier or distributor sell into the region, and what checks does it apply?
  • If you use the Middle Corridor, who controls your goods at each handover?
