Is Bulgaria a Balkan Tiger? What it takes, who did it, and what is missing

Bulgaria now has the anchors past tigers had (EU market, euro, low taxes), but not the trust that turned those anchors into a decade of fast growth; geography, from the Silk Road to the Belt and Road, has never closed that gap by itself.

October 2026 · This article is in English.

The short answer

Not yet. Bulgaria is converging, but at a steady pace rather than a tiger pace. Income per head reached 68 percent of the EU average in purchasing-power terms in 2025, up from roughly 40 percent at EU accession in 2007, and level with Greece for the first time. It is still last in the EU on that measure (bne IntelliNews; Profit.bg, 2007 data).

The anchors are in place. Bulgaria joined the euro on 1 January 2026, a year after full Schengen accession. Public debt is near 30 percent of GDP, and its 10 percent flat tax on corporate and personal income is the lowest in the EU. Yet the European Commission, OECD, World Bank and EBRD all put 2026 growth at 2.5 to 2.7 percent. A tiger run needs something closer to 5 to 6 percent a year, held for a decade.

This piece asks four questions. What makes a tiger? Who has actually done it in the last 30 years, and how did it end? Why has Bulgaria's geography, on the Silk Road then and the Belt and Road now, not been enough? And what would have to change?

What it takes to be a tiger

"Tiger" is a label, not a statistic. We use it to mean an economy that grows at least twice as fast as its richer neighbours for a decade or more, and keeps most of the gain afterwards. The second half of that definition matters. Several famous tigers grew fast and then gave back years of progress in a single crash.

The best evidence on what sustained fast growth requires comes from the World Bank's Commission on Growth and Development. It studied the 13 economies that grew at 7 percent a year or more for at least 25 years after 1950 (IMF Finance & Development). It found five shared features (our summary of the report). The table below sets Bulgaria against each one; the scoring is our own judgement.

The list also leaves out something the studies take for granted: a state, or a partner, that investors trust to keep its promises for ten years.

Two patterns stand out. First, the tigers that kept their gains (Poland, Lithuania, Estonia after 2010) were the ones where institutions improved as fast as the economy. Second, the ones that crashed (Ireland after 2007, Latvia in 2009) grew on credit and property in the second half of the run, after the export engine had done its work.

Bulgaria's own near-tiger run, 2000 to 2008

Bulgaria has already tried once. Growth averaged about 5.6 percent a year from 2000 to 2008, and over 6 percent in the boom years. But that run looked more like late-stage Latvia than early Ireland. Private credit rose from 36 percent of GDP in 2004 to 67 percent in 2007. FDI reached roughly 20 to 30 percent of GDP in 2007, depending on the source, and much of it went into property and finance. The current-account deficit reached an estimated 25 percent of GDP in 2008 (IMF, 2008; wiiw). The crisis ended it. The lesson is that money arriving was never Bulgaria's problem. What the money built was.

From the Silk Road to the Belt and Road: why geography was never enough

Bulgaria's location has been sold as its destiny for a very long time. History suggests caution.

Then. On the old Silk Road, the Bulgarian lands were a corridor, not a terminus. Eastern goods reached them at the end of long chains that ran through Constantinople. The great profits went to whoever controlled the rules of the market, which is why the best-recorded trade episode in medieval Bulgarian history is a dispute about market access: the 894 relocation of Bulgarian merchants from Constantinople to Thessaloniki. Our Silk Road article covers this in detail.

Recently. China's Belt and Road Initiative was explicitly presented as a new Silk Road. Bulgaria was one of the first EU states to sign a Belt and Road memorandum, in November 2015, and hosted the China-Central and Eastern Europe summit in Sofia in 2018 (Bulgarian Ministry of Foreign Affairs). But the flagship money went elsewhere: COSCO took control of Piraeus in Greece, Serbia received the Smederevo steel plant and most of the Belgrade-Budapest railway, and Hungary attracted large Chinese battery and car plants (CSIS). Bulgaria's largest cumulative investors remain the Netherlands, Austria and Germany (BNR, citing BNB data). Bulgaria's one large Chinese-linked industrial project, the Great Wall car assembly plant near Lovech, stopped production in 2016 and its operator filed for bankruptcy in 2017 (Sofia Globe).

Now. Being bypassed by the Belt and Road has turned out to be less of a loss than it looked. The EU's mood has shifted from "connect" to "de-risk". Italy formally left the Belt and Road in December 2023, and Lithuania, Latvia and Estonia left the China-CEE format in 2021 and 2022 (Real Instituto Elcano; LRT). Belgrade-Budapest was still limited to freight in mid-2026 because its Chinese-supplied signalling lacked full certification (Railway News). The route that now matters is the Middle Corridor through Central Asia, the Caspian, the Caucasus and the Black Sea. It is EU-backed, smaller, and full of sanctions-circumvention risk.

The pattern across all three periods is the same. A crossroads earns its value from the rules and trust it can offer, not from the traffic that passes through. Bulgaria was close to the Silk Road but not its centre. It signed up to the Belt and Road but received little of it. Today it sits at the European end of the Middle Corridor. Whether that becomes a growth engine depends on whether Western buyers, banks and insurers trust what crosses Bulgarian territory.

Four binding constraints

  • Rule of law and state capture. Bulgaria scored 40 out of 100 on Transparency International's 2025 Corruption Perceptions Index. That is its worst result since 2012 and joint last in the EU with Hungary (BTA). The cost is not that foreign capital stays away. It is that the capital that comes tends to be short-term or politically connected, rather than the long-term investment that raises productivity.
  • Demographics. The population has fallen from about 7.9 million in 1989 to 6.42 million at the end of 2025. The OECD projects the working-age population to shrink by almost a third by 2060. Returning citizens are a small counter-trend, at roughly 21,000 a year in 2019 to 2023.
  • Productivity and investment. Much of manufacturing is still assembly, and IT leans toward outsourcing rather than owning products and intellectual property. See our notes on automotive and electronics and software and IT.
  • Fiscal slippage. Inside the euro, the budget is the only stabiliser left. The Commission forecasts deficits above 4 percent of GDP in 2026 and 2027. Persistent deficits would eventually force a choice between raising the flat taxes that underpin the investment case and cutting public investment.

Where growth would have to come from

A tiger run needs one or two sectors that can absorb large amounts of capital and export at scale. The strongest candidates are:

  • Automotive and industrial electronics, concentrated around Plovdiv, Sofia and the Thracian plain. It is the largest manufacturing employer among foreign investors, but under pressure from Europe's car slump. See our sector note.
  • Defence and dual-use manufacturing, where existing plants meet EU and NATO demand. See our defence outlook.
  • Software and AI, the best-documented growth sector, now with an EU AI factory planned in Sofia.
  • Nuclear and grid energy, and Black Sea logistics linked to the Middle Corridor.

Defence shows both sides. The October 2025 Rheinmetall ammunition project was announced as a joint venture worth about one billion euros. In July 2026 the new economy minister said there was "no founding agreement" and no joint venture, that Bulgaria's share was not funded, and that talks would restart from scratch (bne IntelliNews). The previous prime minister maintains that a shareholder agreement was signed. Both views describe the same problem: a flagship investment whose legal status depends on who is in government.

How Bulgaria could become a tiger

None of this is a secret. The tigers above suggest six things that would change the trajectory.

  1. Make institutions credible to a ten-year investor. Faster, predictable courts for commercial disputes; a working anti-corruption body; procurement data that is open by default. This is the one change that every other item depends on.
  2. Land two or three anchor investors and their supply chains. Ireland had Intel; Vietnam has Samsung. An anchor brings Tier-1 and Tier-2 suppliers with it. Bulgaria's candidates are automotive electronics, defence and compute.
  3. Turn the corridor into a compliant corridor. Faster border crossings and rail upgrades matter, but so does provenance: goods that can show where they came from will be welcome in the EU; goods that cannot will be stopped or priced out.
  4. Solve labour supply. Diaspora return, managed immigration, and vocational training tied to employers, not only universities.
  5. Absorb EU money on time. Euro membership removes currency risk; slow absorption of EU funds wastes the advantage.
  6. Avoid the second half of the old tiger story. Keep growth export- and investment-led. A credit and property boom on top of the euro would repeat 2007.

Three scenarios

The figures below are illustrative arithmetic, not forecasts. They start from 68 percent of EU income per head in 2025 and assume the EU grows 1.2 percent a year per person.

  • Stagnant convergence: about 2.0 percent a year, reaching roughly 74 percent of the EU level by 2035. Reform stalls, emigration resumes, and investment shifts to Romania, Serbia or Poland.
  • Steady catch-up: about 3.5 percent, reaching roughly 85 percent. Partial judicial reform, EU funds absorbed, big projects delivered late. This is our central case.
  • Tiger: about 5.5 percent, reaching parity. Credible rule of law, diaspora return and managed immigration, anchor investment in electronics, defence and compute, and nuclear new-build on schedule.

Why this is where independent advisory sits

The gap between Bulgaria's steady case and its tiger case is not mainly about roads, ports or tax rates. It is about trust: whether a foreign buyer, investor or insurer can rely on what a Bulgarian counterparty says about who owns it, what it makes, where its goods go and which licences it holds.

Countries close that gap in two ways. The state builds credible institutions, which takes a decade or more. Meanwhile, the private sector builds the trust infrastructure that lets good firms prove they are good: audits, verification, registries that are actually checked, and advisers who are paid by one side only and have nothing to gain from a deal closing. Ireland's IDA did part of that job from the state side. The rest falls to private firms whose only product is a credible, independent check.

Regulation is moving the same way. Large EU companies must comply with the Corporate Sustainability Due Diligence Directive from July 2029, and their due-diligence duties reach suppliers through contracts (iPoint, on Omnibus I). Bulgaria's own foreign-investment screening regime has been operational since July 2025 (DPC). Sanctions enforcement on Middle Corridor trade is tightening. Each of these turns "can you prove it?" into a commercial question.

That is the intersection this firm is built for. We work where Western demand for proof meets Bulgarian and Eastern firms that need to supply it. We do not sell access or influence, and we take no success fees, because a verifier who profits from the deal closing is just another fixer. A Bulgaria that is easy to verify is a Bulgaria that can attract the long-term capital a tiger needs.

What this means for buyers and counterparties

Our reading is that the central case is steady catch-up, and the difference between that and the tiger case is institutional, not geographic. For anyone trading with or investing in Bulgaria, that points to practical questions:

  • How much of a supplier's strength depends on one state contract or one foreign customer?
  • Can its ownership and control be verified in a registry, not asserted in a brochure?
  • Does its business rely on cost advantages that wage growth, which has outpaced productivity, is eroding?
  • If the government changes, which of its contracts, licences or joint ventures are exposed?
  • If it trades with Turkey, the Caucasus or Central Asia, can it document origin and end use well enough for an EU bank or insurer?
