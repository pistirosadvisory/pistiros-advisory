Public procurement in Bulgaria for foreign bidders

EU, defence and energy money is flowing into Bulgarian public contracts, and foreign companies are entitled to bid on equal terms. In practice, competition is thin in many tenders, and the procedural details trip up newcomers. This note covers how the system works, where it goes wrong, and how to read a tender before spending money on a bid.

October 2026 · This article is in English.

How the system works

Public procurement in Bulgaria follows the EU procurement directives, implemented through the national Public Procurement Act. The main points for a foreign bidder:

  • One platform. Procedures are run through the Centralised Automated Information System for Electronic Public Procurement, known by its Bulgarian initials as ЦАИС ЕОП (CAIS EOP). Tender documents are published there and bids are submitted there.
  • EU publication. Contracts above EU value thresholds are also announced on the EU's Tenders Electronic Daily (TED), which is often the easiest place for foreign firms to find them.
  • Electronic signature. Bids are signed electronically; check early which qualified electronic signatures the platform accepts for foreign signatories.
  • Self-declaration. Bidders declare that they meet exclusion and selection criteria using the European Single Procurement Document (in Bulgarian, ЕЕДОП), with supporting certificates requested later, usually from the winner.
  • Language. Tender documents and bids are in Bulgarian. Foreign certificates and documents usually need certified translation.
  • Appeals. Challenges to tender conditions and award decisions go to the Commission for Protection of Competition, with short deadlines.

Procedural details change; confirm the current rules in the Public Procurement Act and the platform's guidance before bidding.

Where it goes wrong

Bulgaria's procurement system has a long record of weak competition:

  • In 2022 the head of the Public Procurement Agency said more than a quarter of procedures had a single bidder (BTA).
  • A Corruption Risk Index pilot by Bulgarian civil-society researchers, covering 166 construction procedures run by district centres between January 2020 and June 2023, found about 60% received only one bid and scored overall corruption risk at 23.75% on a 0–100% scale. Single-bid rates in some municipalities were far higher: 77.9% in Burgas and 100% in two small municipalities (BILI). This was a pilot sample in one sector, not a national statistic.

The same research described two common patterns: key documents such as the evaluation methodology missing from the published file, and selection criteria loose enough to allow subjective judgement of who qualifies.

A single bidder is not proof of corruption. It can reflect a small market or a short deadline. But it is a reason to ask whether the tender was written for one company.

Reading a tender before you bid

  • Who wrote the specification? Brand names, unusually narrow technical requirements or past-performance criteria that only one firm meets are warnings.
  • Is the evaluation method published? If the scoring is not in the file, you cannot price your bid or challenge the result.
  • How long is the deadline? A short window on a complex contract favours bidders who knew it was coming.
  • What is the history? Search the platform for previous tenders by the same authority for the same work. Who won, and how many bids were there?
  • Who are the likely competitors? Check their ownership and links to the authority's management.
  • Is the money secure? For EU-funded contracts, check the programme and any audit findings; for state companies, check the counterparty's finances.

Partnering with Bulgarian firms

Many foreign bidders team up with a local partner for language, licences and local presence. That is legitimate and often sensible. It is also where risk enters. A partner chosen for its political connections rather than its capability exposes you to bribery law in your home country as well as in Bulgaria. UK and US anti-bribery laws apply to conduct abroad, and to payments made through intermediaries.

Check a partner as you would a supplier: ownership, management, filed accounts, past contracts, and any links to the contracting authority.

Red flags

  • A consultant who offers to "arrange" a tender result, or whose fee depends on winning.
  • Requests for payments to third parties not involved in the work.
  • A partner whose owner or relative sits on the contracting authority's board or evaluation committee.
  • Tender terms changed after informal contact with one bidder.
  • An authority that discourages questions or clarifications.

What this means for bidders

  • Have you found the tender on TED or CAIS EOP early enough to prepare properly?
  • Does the specification read as if written for one competitor, and is a challenge worthwhile?
  • Can you sign electronically and provide translated, certified documents in time?
  • Have you vetted your Bulgarian partner and every intermediary as carefully as a supplier?
  • Is your fee arrangement with any adviser fixed, transparent and independent of the result?
